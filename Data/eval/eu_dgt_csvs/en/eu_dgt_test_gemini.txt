State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
In the following, the Supervisory Authority will assess whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are fulfilled for (i) the scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation and (ii) the five investment agreements.
The classification as state aid requires that an advantage is granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of the state aid rules, the term 'State' covers both regional and local bodies.
A loss of tax revenue is an expenditure of state funds, as in the case of tax expenditures; state aid can be granted not only through tax laws, regulations, and administrative provisions, but also through the practice of the tax administration.
A reduction of the tax base or a full or partial reduction of the tax amount, fees, or levies implies a loss of revenue and is therefore equivalent to the use of government funds in the form of tax expenditures.

Favoring certain companies or branches of industry.
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, since only undertakings investing in certain areas in Iceland which are eligible for aid pursuant to Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement are entitled to corresponding aid.
The Surveillance Authority accepts the argument of the Icelandic authorities that the changes to the scheme accompanying the supplementary regulation increase the benefits granted by the scheme for Becromal.
The amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation therefore constitute a selective measure, as they only affect a specific company.
Accordingly, the Supervisory Authority concludes that the supplementary regulation constitutes the favouring of certain undertakings or branches of production within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
The beneficiaries of aid under the five investment agreements are also individual undertakings, and the measures are therefore selective.
The concept of state aid is broader than that of a subsidy, as it covers not only positive benefits, such as subsidies themselves, but also state measures which, in various forms, reduce the charges that an undertaking normally has to bear and which, although not constituting subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are equivalent to them in their character and effect.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which public authorities grant certain undertakings an exemption from a charge, which, although not involving a transfer of state resources, places the beneficiaries in a more favourable financial position than other taxpayers, constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allow the beneficiaries to be exempted from a portion of the costs that they would normally have to bear themselves in the course of their business activities, such as the payment of the applicable corporate tax at a specific time, the statutory municipal property tax, and the generally applicable social security contributions.
The Supervisory Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and sectors of production within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortions of competition and impairment of trade between the contracting parties
According to settled case law, the classification of a national measure as state aid does not require proof of an actual effect of the aid on trade between Member States and an actual distortion of competition, but only an examination of whether the aid is liable to affect such trade or distort competition.
Where aid granted by an EFTA Member State strengthens the position of an undertaking vis-à-vis other competitors in trade within the EEA, that trade must be considered to be affected by the aid.
In its Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme has strengthened the competitive position of the supported undertakings in the eligible areas vis-à-vis actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position against other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
Becromal has confirmed that the market for anode foils in aluminum electrolytic capacitors is a global market and that Becromal's customers include companies from […] and […].
However, the fact that Becromal's biggest competitors are companies from […] does not mean that the agreement has no effect on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement strengthens Verne's competitive position in relation to other data centers in Iceland.
In addition, Verne operates a global data center for the wholesale market, whose services are available to customers in the EEA as well as on the global market.
The Kísilfélagið investment agreement strengthens the competitive position of Kísilfélagið vis-à-vis other manufacturers of metallurgical silicon and silica fume.
The market for metallurgical silicon and for silica fume is a global market.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens Thorsil's competitive position against other manufacturers of silicon metal.
According to the preamble of this investment agreement, the project was launched, among other things, to meet the increased demand for silicon metal in Europe and North America.
The GMR-Endurvinnslan investment agreement strengthens GMR Endurvinnslan's competitive position against other recycling companies and steel producers.
The company intends to deliver the majority of its products to buyers in the construction industry.
Accordingly, the supervisory authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of each of the supported companies in the eligible areas vis-à-vis their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.

In its opening decision, the Surveillance Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements was granted in accordance with the scheme approved by the Surveillance Authority, as the agreements, on the face of it, had been affected by amendments to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation.
According to Part II, Article 1(c) of Protocol 3, amendments to existing aid are to be considered as new aid.
According to Article 4(1) of Decision No 195/04/COL of the Surveillance Authority on the implementing provisions of Part II of Article 27 of Protocol 3 (the ‘Implementing Decision’), ‘[…] any change, other than a change of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market, shall be regarded as an alteration to existing aid’.
Changes that do not affect the fundamental nature of the aid therefore do not influence the classification of the measure.
The changes made by the supplementary regulation allow aid to be granted for projects that had already started before the aid was granted, and even before the regulation entered into force.
The changes made by the supplementary regulation alter the fundamental characteristics of the regulation.
In particular, the requirement of an incentive effect was removed from the regulation.
This requirement constituted an essential part of the compatibility assessment for State aid measures under the originally approved scheme (see the description in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Accordingly, the supervisory authority is of the opinion that these amendments to the regulation, which were made by the supplementary regulation, constitute a fundamental change to the originally approved regulation.
In view of the fact that, pursuant to Part II, Article 1(c) of Protocol 3, amendments to existing aid are to be considered new aid, the question arises as to whether the amended existing aid as a whole, or only the amendments, are to be classified as new aid.
In its judgment in the Gibraltar case, the Court of First Instance ruled:
Consequently, the supervisory authority must assess whether the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation can be separated from the original regulation.
If this is the case, the changes constitute new aid and must therefore be reassessed in light of Article 61(3)(c) and the Surveillance Authority's Guidelines on National Regional Aid for 2007-2013 (the 'Regional Aid Guidelines 2007').
According to established case law, an amendment to an existing aid scheme which extends the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries is clearly severable from the original scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the original scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3(c) of the Investment Incentive Regulation states that aid can also be granted for a project that was started before the entry into force of the supplementary regulation but after the conclusion of a special investment agreement.
This amendment extended the scope of the scheme beyond that established in Decision No 390/10/COL of the Surveillance Authority, as aid could thus also be granted for projects that had already been started before the scheme entered into force and would therefore have failed the test regarding the incentive effect laid down in the approved scheme.
The supplementary regulation thus extended the provision to a new category of beneficiaries.
Furthermore, the Surveillance Authority takes the view that the amendments to Articles 8 and 20 of the Investment Aid Regulation, introduced by the supplementary regulation, i.e. the reduction of the corporate tax reference rate and the extension of the maximum duration of the tax exemption, were introduced solely to allow this new category of beneficiaries to benefit from the scheme.
These new beneficiaries can thus receive more aid than was the case under the originally approved scheme.
For this reason, the changes to the regulation are fundamentally interconnected.
However, these changes do not affect the compatibility of individual measures that have already been introduced under the originally approved scheme.
The supervisory authority therefore considers these changes accompanying the supplementary regulation to be changes that can be clearly separated from the original regulation.
Consequently, the Supervisory Authority is of the opinion that these amendments to the scheme constitute a new aid measure.
Furthermore, it follows that any aid granted on the basis of the supplementary regulation is also to be considered new aid.
The supervisory authority is therefore assessing the compatibility of the amendments to the regulation introduced by the supplementary regulation.

Becromal could therefore have passed the test of the incentive effect prescribed by the regulation solely on the basis of the changes introduced by the supplementary regulation.

For the sake of completeness and in order to address the arguments put forward by Becromal and the Icelandic authorities, the Surveillance Authority has alternatively assessed the Becromal investment agreement as an ad hoc measure.
To this end, the supervisory authority refers to the Regional Aid Guidelines 2007 and in particular to the condition contained therein regarding the incentive effect.
According to point 30 of the 2007 Guidelines on regional aid, the presence of an incentive effect is an essential condition for the compatibility of the aid:
The purpose of the provisions in point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines is to enable the supervisory authority to verify whether an investment aid creates a sufficient incentive effect, without undertaking a full analysis of the economic preconditions at the time of the beneficiary's investment decision.
Should such a review not be guaranteed before work on the project commences, "the entire project can receive no aid," and is therefore ineligible for funding.
As confirmed by the Court in its judgment in the Kronoply case, the Surveillance Authority may rely on a temporal sequence as a basis for its assessment of the incentive effect.
Becromal argued that the legal assessment of state aid cases had changed with the introduction of a refined economic analysis and that the Surveillance Authority, when assessing the incentive effect, must therefore also evaluate its economic nature (i.e. whether the aid contributed to a change in the aid recipient's behaviour) and, in doing so, also observe the provisions of the LIP Guidelines.
The supervisory authority cannot follow this argument.
Becromal refers to the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: ‘[…] the Court of Justice, in its review of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, ruled that the sole decisive factor in determining whether the aid had an incentive effect was whether it had altered the investors’ conduct, and disregarded all the references made by the Court of First Instance to the Kronoply case’.
In its interpretation of the judgment in the HGA case, however, Becromal disregards the Court's express opinion that the finding that '[...] the requirement that the aid application must precede the start of the implementation of the aid is a simple, objective and appropriate criterion enabling the Commission to presume the necessity of the planned aid' was not called into question on appeal.
The Supervisory Authority therefore takes the view that it is appropriate in this case to base the finding of incompatibility on a sequence of events, namely that i) the examination of the incentive effect prescribed in the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines was not carried out and ii) work on the project had already commenced before the investment agreement was concluded and before the aid was granted.
The Supervisory Authority furthermore rejects Becromal's argument that the Supervisory Authority must examine the project in the light of the LIP Guidelines and apply the incentive effect test described therein.
The Surveillance Authority states that for certain large investment projects, the assessment of the incentive effect in accordance with point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application for aid is submitted and eligibility is established before work begins) is supplemented by a second, economic assessment (based on an economic analysis of the contribution of the aid to a change in the aid beneficiary's behaviour) in accordance with point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines and the LIP Guidelines.
The LIP guidelines provide for an in-depth review of certain large investment projects, i.e., those that have a significant impact on trade and could lead to major distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the LIP Guidelines and point 49 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment with eligible expenditure of over EUR 50 million.
As previously mentioned, the Becromal investment agreement covers investment costs totaling EUR 117.25 million for both phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have submitted information to the Surveillance Authority regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs for the project.
Furthermore, none of these parties has submitted information to the supervisory authority on the conditions for the detailed examination obligation under the LIP Guidelines, to which point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines also refer (i.e. that the aid beneficiary accounts for more than 25% of the sales of the product concerned on the relevant market and that the capacity created by the investment project represents more than 5% of the market for the product concerned).
The Supervisory Authority is of the view that there is no indication that the Becromal project exceeds the thresholds laid down in point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines.
The supervisory authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis described in the LIP guidelines is not applicable to the Becromal investment agreement.
Consequently, according to established case-law and the wording of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, it is sufficient to establish whether the aid beneficiary has submitted an application for aid; furthermore, the competent authority must confirm the measure in writing before work begins, however, the aid is conditional upon approval by the supervisory authority.
According to the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, 'start of works' means 'the start of construction work or the first legally binding commitment to order equipment, excluding feasibility studies'.
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work on the Becromal project began in 2008 and thus before the signing of the previous agreement on 7 July 2009.
The Supervisory Authority cannot accept the argument put forward by Becromal that the ‘start of works’ should be limited to the ordering of plant specifically manufactured for the project in question, and that the acquisition of land and/or the construction of a factory does not necessarily constitute an investment decision, as such an investment could easily be resold at any time.
This interpretation is supported by the case-law of the Court, according to which even small investments by the aid recipient are sufficient to produce a legal effect; they thus constitute the first phase of the works.
Furthermore, the information available to the supervisory authority indicates that Becromal began construction of the plant in early 2008 and that the recruitment process was started later that year.

According to the Icelandic authorities, a draft investment agreement had been initialed (but not signed) on October 23, 2009.
This is why the Icelandic authorities argued that there had been no prior investment agreement with Verne that could constitute a "special investment agreement" within the meaning of the supplementary regulation.
However, the Authority also observed that, at the time, it did not have sufficient information regarding the status of this project.
In their response, the Icelandic authorities stated that:
Construction work on the Becromal project began following the investment agreement concluded on 29 May 2009, which was prior to the conclusion of the Becromal investment agreement on 30 December 2010.

State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
The Authority will hereinafter examine whether the criteria for State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are met for i) the scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation and ii) the five investment agreements.
To qualify as State aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of applying the rules on State aid, the term 'State' also covers regional and local entities.
A loss of tax revenue is equivalent to the consumption of state resources in the form of tax expenditures, and state aid can be provided both through tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory, or administrative nature and through the practices of the tax administration.
A reduction of the tax base or a total or partial reduction of the amount of tax, duties, or charges, implies a loss of revenue and is therefore equivalent to the consumption of state resources in the form of tax expenditures.
The regime as amended by the supplementary regulation.
The Authority had concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme constituted a transfer of State resources.
The modifications made by the supplementary regulation do not alter this conclusion.
In particular, the five investment agreements contained the following tax and charge waivers:
the maximum corporate tax rate for the company that would invest in the project concerned was fixed at the rate in force on the date of the signing of an agreement between the company and the Ministry of Industry;

the depreciation of assets to a zero residual value, in lieu of the 10% tax provided for by Law no. 90/2003; and

Now, the leasing of land or real estate at a price below market value implies a loss of revenue and therefore constitutes a transfer of State resources.

Measures favoring certain companies or certain productions.
The Authority concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme was selective as only undertakings investing in certain regions of Iceland eligible for aid under Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement could benefit from aid under it.
The Authority accepts the argument of the Icelandic authorities that the amendments made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation extend to Becromal the benefits granted under the scheme.
These changes therefore constitute a selective measure, since they concern a particular company.
The Authority concludes, therefore, that the supplementary regulation favours certain undertakings and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Similarly, since the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are specific companies, the measures are selective.
The concept of aid is broader than that of a subsidy, given that it includes not only positive benefits, such as subsidies themselves, but also State interventions which, in various forms, alleviate the charges normally borne by an undertaking's budget and which, thereby, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are of the same nature and have identical effects.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which public authorities grant certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although not involving a transfer of State resources, places the beneficiaries in a more favourable financial position than other taxpayers constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allowed the beneficiaries to be discharged from part of the costs that they would normally have had to bear themselves in the course of their business, such as the corporate tax applicable at a given time, the normal municipal property tax, and the generally applicable social security contribution.
The Authority concludes, therefore, that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the contracting parties
It is settled case-law that for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to establish an actual effect of the aid on trade between the contracting parties and an actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether the aid is liable to affect such trade and to distort competition.
Furthermore, where aid granted by an EFTA State strengthens the position of an undertaking in relation to other competing undertakings in intra-EEA trade, the latter must be considered to be affected by the aid.
The Authority had concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the beneficiary undertakings in the eligible regions compared to that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The investment agreement concluded with Becromal strengthens this company's competitive position compared to that of other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As confirmed by Becromal, the market for anode foils used in aluminium electrolytic capacitors is a global market, and Becromal's customers include companies […] and […].
Also, the fact that Becromal's main competitors are companies […] does not mean that there is no effect on trade within the EEA.
The investment agreement concluded with Verne strengthens this company's competitive position compared to that of other data centers established in Iceland.
In addition, Verne operates a global wholesale data center whose services will be made available to customers within the EEA and on the global market.
The investment agreement concluded with Kísilfélagið strengthens this company's competitive position compared to that of other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica fume.
The market for metallurgical silicon and silica fume is global.
The investment agreement concluded with Thorsil strengthens the company's competitive position compared to that of other silicon metal producers.
As indicated in the preamble of the said investment agreement, the project was launched in particular to meet the increase in demand for silicon metal in Europe and North America.
The investment agreement concluded with GMR Endurvinnslan strengthens this company's competitive position compared to that of other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
This company plans to export the majority of its products to sell to buyers in the construction sector.
Consequently, the Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions compared to that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.

According to Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, 'any modification of existing aid' shall be considered as new aid.
Pursuant to Article 4(1) of the Authority's Decision No 195/04/COL on the implementing measures referred to in Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (the ‘Implementing Decision’), ‘[…] “alteration of existing aid” shall mean any change, other than changes of a purely formal or administrative nature which are not liable to influence the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market’.
Therefore, modifications that do not concern the substance of the aid do not affect how the measure is to be classified.
The amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation provide that aid may be granted for projects where work began before the aid was granted — in fact, even before the scheme entered into force.
The changes made by the supplementary regulation modify the fundamental characteristics of the scheme.
In particular, the requirement for an incentive effect provided for by the scheme has been removed.

Consequently, the Authority considers that the amendments made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation constitute a substantial modification of the scheme as authorized.

Point 30 of the 2007 guidelines is intended to enable the Authority to verify whether the investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without conducting a comprehensive examination of all the economic aspects of the aid beneficiary's investment decision at the time it was made.

Thus, as the Court confirmed in the Kronoply judgment, the Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect on a chronological circumstance.
Becromal argued that the assessment of State aid cases has evolved with the introduction of a more refined economic analysis, so that, when assessing the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also evaluate its economic nature (i.e., verifying whether it contributed to a change in the beneficiary's behaviour), while also taking into account the provisions of the guidelines on large investment projects.
The Authority cannot accept this argument.
Becromal invokes the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: “[...] the Court, in its review of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, held that the sole determining factor as to whether the aid measure had an incentive effect consisted in examining whether it had changed the investor's behaviour and set aside all the references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case.”
However, Becromal's interpretation of the HGA judgment fails to take into account the fact that the Court of Justice expressly found that the General Court's conclusion that ‘[…] the fact that the application for aid predates the start of work on the investment project constitutes a simple, relevant and adequate criterion enabling the Commission to presume the necessity of the proposed aid’ was not challenged on appeal.
The Authority therefore considers that, in this case, its finding of incompatibility should be based on a chronological assessment: more specifically, i) the incentive effect criterion set out in the 2007 guidelines was not met and ii) work began on the project before the investment agreement was concluded and the aid was granted.
The Authority also rejects Becromal's argument that the Authority must examine the project in light of the guidelines on large investment projects and apply the incentive effect criterion prescribed therein.
The Authority observes that, for certain large investment projects, the incentive effect criterion set out in point 30 of the 2007 guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application has been submitted and eligibility confirmed before the start of the works) is supplemented by a second, economic criterion (based on an economic analysis of whether the aid contributes to a change in the beneficiary's behaviour), set out in point 57 of the guidelines on regional aid and in the guidelines on large investment projects.
The guidelines on major investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain major investment projects, namely those that have substantial effects on trade and may lead to significant distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the guidelines on large investment projects and point 49 of the 2007 guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment whose eligible expenditure exceeds EUR 50,000,000.
As previously indicated, the investment agreement concluded with Becromal mentions an amount of EUR 117,250,000 as the total investment cost for the two phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have provided information to the Authority on the aid intensity or the eligible costs relating to the project.
Similarly, they have provided no information on the conditions for the detailed verification requirement laid down in the guidelines on large investment projects, referred to in point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 Guidelines (namely that the aid beneficiary accounts for more than 25% of the sales of the products concerned on the relevant market and that the capacity created by the project represents more than 5% of the market for the product concerned).
According to the Authority, there is no indication that the Becromal project exceeds the thresholds set out in point 57 of the 2007 guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis provided for in the guidelines on major investment projects does not apply to the investment agreement concluded with Becromal.
Therefore, in view of the case-law and the wording of the 2007 guidelines, it is sufficient to verify whether an application for aid has been submitted by the beneficiary and whether written confirmation has been given by the granting authority, subject to the authorisation of the measure by the Authority, before work on the project began.
According to the 2007 guidelines, the expression "start of works" means "either the start of construction works, or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding preliminary feasibility studies."
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work had begun on the Becromal project in 2008, even before the signing of the prior agreement of 7 July 2009.

To qualify as state aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through state resources.
For the purposes of applying the rules on State aid, the term 'State' also covers regional and local bodies.

A reduction in the tax base or a total or partial reduction in taxes, fees, or charges implies a loss of tax revenue and is therefore equivalent to the consumption of state resources in the form of tax expenditures.

Favoring certain companies or productions
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, in the sense that only undertakings investing in certain regions of Iceland and eligible for aid pursuant to Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement could receive aid under it.
The Authority accepts the argument of the Icelandic authorities that the amendments to the scheme resulting from the supplementary Regulation expanded the advantages granted to Becromal under the scheme.
Therefore, these modifications constitute a selective measure, since they affect a specific company.
The Authority therefore concludes that the supplementary Regulation favours certain companies and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Similarly, the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are individual undertakings and, therefore, the measures are selective.
The definition of aid is more general than that of a subsidy, since it comprises not only positive benefits, such as subsidies properly so-called, but also state interventions which, under various forms, alleviate the burdens that normally fall on a company's budget and which, therefore, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, have a similar character and produce the same effect.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which public authorities grant certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although it does not involve a transfer of state funds, places those to whom the exemption applies in a more favorable financial position than that of other taxpayers, constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allowed the beneficiaries to be released from some of the costs they would normally have had to bear in their business activities, such as corporate tax at the applicable rate, municipal property tax, and general social security contributions.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain companies and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the contracting Parties
According to settled case-law, for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to establish the actual effect of the aid on trade between the member Contracting Parties and the actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether such aid is liable to affect such trade and distort competition.
Furthermore, where aid granted by an EFTA State strengthens the position of an undertaking vis-à-vis other undertakings competing with it in intra-EEA trade, such trade must be considered to be affected by the aid.
The Authority concluded in Decision 390/10/COL that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions, compared to their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position with respect to the other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As Becromal has confirmed, the market for anodized foils used in aluminum electrolytic capacitors is global, and among Becromal's customers are companies in […] and […].
Thus, the fact that Becromal's main competitors are companies from […] does not mean that there are no effects on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement reinforces Verne's competitive position with respect to other data centers in Iceland.
In addition, Verne manages a global wholesale data center, whose services will be available to clients from the EEA and the global market.
The Kísilfélagið investment agreement strengthens Kísilfélagið's competitive position with respect to the other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica fume.
The metallurgical silicon and silica powder market is global.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens Thorsil's competitive position with respect to the other silicon metal producers.
As noted in the preamble to the Thorsil investment agreement, the project was initiated, among other things, to meet an increase in demand for silicon metal from Europe and North America.
The GMR Endurvinnslan investment agreement strengthens GMR Endurvinnslan's competitive position with respect to other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
The company intends to export the majority of its products to buyers in the construction sector.
Consequently, the Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions in comparison with their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.

In its opening decision, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements had been granted under the scheme, since the agreements seemed, at first sight, to have been affected by the amendments to the scheme resulting from the Supplementary Regulation.
In accordance with Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid are to be considered as new aid.
In accordance with Article 4(1) of the Authority's Decision No 195/04/COL on the implementing provisions of Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (hereinafter referred to as the 'Implementing Decision'), "[...] 'alteration of existing aid' shall mean any change, other than a change of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market".
Therefore, modifications that do not affect the essence of the aid do not affect how the measure should be classified.
The modifications introduced by the supplementary Regulation allow aid to be granted to projects on which work has started before the aid is awarded, even before the entry into force of the scheme.
The modifications introduced by the supplementary Regulation alter the basic characteristics of the scheme.
In particular, the incentive effect requirement has been abolished.
This requirement was an important part of the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measures granted under the scheme, as initially approved (as described in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Consequently, the Body considers that the amendments to the scheme introduced by the supplementary Regulation constitute a substantial modification of the Scheme as approved.
Whereas, pursuant to Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid are to be considered as new aid, the question arises as to whether the modified existing aid should be considered as new aid, or only the modifications as such.

According to settled case-law, a modification of an existing aid scheme that extends it to a new category of beneficiaries can be clearly dissociated from the initial scheme, as the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the initial scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3(c) of the Regulation on incentives specifies that aid may also be granted to a project that has begun after a special investment agreement has been concluded and before the supplementary Regulation enters into force.

In accordance with paragraph 30 of the 2007 RAG, the existence of an incentive effect is a necessary prerequisite for compatibility:
The objective of paragraph 30 of the 2007 RAG is to enable the Authority to determine whether the investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without conducting a full analysis of the economic circumstances of the beneficiary's investment decision at the time it was made.

Therefore, as confirmed by the General Court in the Kronoply judgment, the Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect by reference to a chronological circumstance.
Becromal has argued that the assessment of state aid matters has changed with the introduction of a more detailed economic analysis, such that when evaluating the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also assess its economic nature (i.e., whether it has contributed to a change in the behavior of the aid beneficiary), also taking into account the provisions of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects.
The Body cannot accept this argument.
Becromal refers to the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: ‘[…] the Court, in its examination of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, establishes that the only factor that determines whether the aid measure had an incentive effect is to examine whether the investor's behaviour has changed, and did not take into account all the references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case’.
However, Becromal's interpretation of the HGA judgment does not take into account the Court of Justice's express statement that the General Court's assessment that ‘[…] the fact that the aid application predates the start of the implementation of the investment project is a simple, relevant and appropriate criterion which allows the Commission to presume the necessity of the planned aid’ was not called into question in the appeal proceedings.
Therefore, the Authority is of the opinion that in this case it is necessary to base its conclusion of incompatibility on a chronological assessment: namely, that (i) the incentive requirement laid down in the 2007 RAG was not met, and (ii) work on the project began before the investment agreement was concluded and the aid was granted.
The Body also rejects Becromal's claim that the Body must examine the project in the light of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects, and apply the incentive effect requirement set out therein.
The Authority notes that, for some large investment projects, the incentive effect test established in point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application has been submitted and eligibility confirmed before work begins) is supplemented by a second test, of an economic nature (based on an economic analysis that the aid contributes to a change in the beneficiary's behaviour), provided for in paragraph 57 of the Guidelines on Regional Aid and in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects.
The Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain large investment projects, namely, those which have significant effects on trade and may give rise to substantive distortions of competition.
According to paragraph 3 of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects and paragraph 49 of the 2007 RAG, a large investment project is defined as an initial investment whose eligible costs exceed EUR 50 million.
As previously mentioned, the Becromal investment agreement refers to EUR 117.25 million as the total investment cost for the two phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have submitted any information to the Authority regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs of the project.
Nor have they provided information on the conditions for the detailed verification obligation laid down in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects referred to in point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 RAG (i.e. that the aid beneficiary holds more than 25% of the products in question on the relevant market, and that the production capacity created by the project accounts for more than 5% of the relevant product market).
In the opinion of the Authority, nothing indicates that the Becromal project exceeds the maximum limits set out in paragraph 57 of the 2007 Guidelines.
The Body concludes, therefore, that the in-depth analysis set out in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects does not apply to the Becromal investment agreement.
Therefore, in view of the case-law and the wording of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, it is sufficient to verify whether the application for aid has been submitted by the beneficiary and whether the granting authority has issued a written confirmation, conditional on the approval of the measure by the Authority, before work on the project began.
The 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines define the term 'start of works' as 'the start of construction works, or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding preliminary feasibility studies'.
As the Icelandic authorities and Becromal have confirmed, construction work on the Becromal project began in 2008, even before the signing of the previous agreement of 7 July 2009.
The Authority cannot accept Becromal's argument that the 'start of works' should be limited to orders for equipment specially designed for the project in question, and that the acquisition of land or the construction of a plant does not necessarily signal the beginning of an investment decision, since such an investment could easily be sold at any given time.
This interpretation is corroborated by the case-law of the General Court, according to which even a low level of investment by the beneficiary is sufficient to produce legal effects, and is therefore considered to constitute the first phase of the works.
On the other hand, the information available to the Authority shows that Becromal began construction of the plant in early 2008, and its procurement procedure was initiated at the end of that year.
