State aid within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement

In the following, the Authority will assess whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement are met for (i) the scheme as amended by the Supplementary Regulation and (ii) the five investment agreements.
The classification as State aid presupposes that an advantage is granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of the State aid rules, the concept of 'State' covers both regional and local bodies.
A loss of tax revenue is a consumption of public funds, as in the case of tax expenditure; public support may be provided not only through tax laws, regulations and administrative provisions, but also through the practice of tax administration.
A reduction in the tax base or a reduction in the amount of tax, fees or charges, in whole or in part, implies a loss of revenue and therefore amounts to the use of State resources in the form of tax expenditure.

Favouring certain undertakings or the production of certain goods
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, as only undertakings investing in certain areas in Iceland eligible for aid under Article 61 (3) (c) of the EEA Agreement are eligible for the aid.

Accordingly, the Authority concludes that the Supplementary Regulation constitutes an advantage to certain undertakings or product groups within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement.
The beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are also individual companies and the measures are therefore selective.
The concept of State aid is broader than that of subsidy, since it includes not only positive benefits such as subsidies themselves, but also State measures which, in various forms, reduce the burden which an undertaking has to bear on a regular basis and which, while not constituting subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are equivalent in nature and effect to subsidies.
According to settled case-law, a measure whereby public authorities grant to certain undertakings an exemption from taxation which, although not involving a transfer of State resources, constitutes a financial advantage to the recipients of the aid over other taxable persons constitutes State aid or aid granted through State resources.
The five investment agreements allow the beneficiaries to be relieved of part of the costs that they would normally have to bear themselves in the course of their business activities, such as the payment of the applicable corporate tax at a certain date, the municipal statutory property tax and the general social security contributions.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and the production of certain goods within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the Contracting Parties
According to settled case-law, the qualification of a national measure as State aid does not require proof of the actual effect of the aid on trade between Member States and of a real distortion of competition, but only of the ability of the aid to affect such trade or to distort competition.
If aid granted by an EFTA State strengthens the position of an undertaking compared with other competitors in intra-EEA trade, the latter must be regarded as affected by the aid.
In its Decision No 390/10/COL, the Authority concluded that the scheme has strengthened the competitive position of the aided companies in the eligible areas vis-à-vis actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position vis-à-vis other producers of anodized aluminium foil for electrolyte capacitors.

In its opening decision, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the Investment Agreements was granted in accordance with the scheme approved by the Authority, as the Agreements appeared to have been affected by changes to the scheme introduced by the Supplementary Regulation.
In accordance with Article 1 (c) of Part II of Protocol 3, changes to existing aid are to be considered as new aid.
According to Article 4 (1) of the Authority's Decision No 195/04/COL on the detailed rules for the implementation of Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (the 'Implementing Decision') [...], any change to an existing aid measure is considered to be a restructuring, except a change of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market.
Changes which do not affect the basic nature of the aid do not therefore affect the classification of the measure.
The amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation allow aid to be granted for projects which have already been started before the aid was granted, or even before the scheme entered into force.
The amendments made by the Supplementary Regulation alter the basic features of the scheme.
In particular, the incentive effect requirement was removed from the scheme.
This condition was an essential element in the assessment of the compatibility of aid measures under the scheme originally approved (see the description in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Accordingly, the Authority considers that these amendments to the scheme introduced by the Supplementary Regulation constitute a fundamental change to the scheme originally approved.
Given that, according to Article 1 (c) of Part II of Protocol 3, changes to existing aid are to be considered as new aid, the question arises as to whether the altered existing aid as a whole or only the changes are to be classified as new aid.
In its judgment in Gibraltar, the Court of First Instance held:
Consequently, the Authority has to assess whether the amendments can be separated from the original scheme by the Supplementary Regulation.
If this is the case, the amendments constitute new aid and must therefore be reassessed in the light of Article 61 (3) (c) and the Authority's Guidelines on State aid for regional development 2007-2013 (the 'Regional Aid Guidelines 2007').
According to settled case-law, an amendment to an existing aid scheme extending the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries is clearly separable from the original scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries has no impact on the assessment of the compatibility of the original scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3 (c) of the Investment Promotion Regulation states that aid may also be granted for a project which was already started before the entry into force of the Supplementary Regulation but after the conclusion of a specific investment agreement.
This amendment extended the scope of the scheme beyond that laid down in the Authority's Decision No 390/10/COL, since it allows aid to be granted for projects which were already started before the entry into force of the scheme and which would therefore not have passed the examination of the incentive effect laid down in the approved scheme.
The Supplementary Regulation thus opened the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries.
Furthermore, the Authority considers that the amendments to Articles 8 and 20 of the Investment Incentive Regulation introduced by the Supplementary Regulation, i.e. the reduction of the reference rate of corporate tax and the extension of the maximum duration of the tax exemption, were introduced only in order to allow this new category of beneficiaries to benefit from this scheme.
These new beneficiaries can therefore receive more aid than under the originally approved scheme.
Therefore, the amendments to the scheme are fundamentally linked.
However, these amendments do not affect the compatibility of individual measures already introduced under the originally approved scheme.
The Authority therefore considers these amendments to the Supplementary Regulation to be amendments that can be clearly separated from the original scheme.
The Authority therefore considers that these changes to the scheme constitute a new aid measure.
It also follows that any aid granted on the basis of the Supplementary Regulation must also be considered as new aid.
The Authority will therefore assess the compatibility of the amendments to the scheme by the Supplementary Regulation.

For the sake of completeness and to address the arguments put forward by Becromal and the Icelandic authorities, the Authority has alternatively assessed the Becromal Investment Agreement as an ad hoc measure.
For this purpose, the Authority refers to the Regional Aid Guidelines 2007 and in particular to the incentive effect condition contained therein.
According to point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, the presence of an incentive effect is an essential condition for the compatibility of the aid:
The provisions in point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines aim to enable the Authority to verify whether an investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect without a full analysis of the economic conditions prevailing at the time of the beneficiary's investment decision.
If such a check is not ensured before work on the project is started, the whole project cannot receive aid and is therefore not eligible.
As confirmed by the General Court in Kronoply, the Authority may rely on a time lapse as a basis for its assessment of the incentive effect.
Becromal argued that the legal assessment of aid cases had changed with the introduction of a refined economic analysis and that the Authority therefore had to assess the economic nature of aid (i.e. whether the aid contributed to a change in the behaviour of the beneficiary) when assessing the incentive effect, also taking into account the provisions of the LIP Guidelines.
The Authority cannot accept this argument.
Becromal refers to the Court of Justice judgment in HGA to argue that: [...] the Court of Justice, in its examination of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, has held that the only decisive factor in determining whether the aid has an incentive effect is whether it has changed the behaviour of investors and has disregarded all the references made by the Court of First Instance to the Kronoply case.
However, in its interpretation of the HGA judgment, Becromal disregards the express opinion of the Court of Justice that 'the requirement that the aid application must precede the start of the implementation of the aid is a simple, factual and appropriate criterion enabling the Commission to suspect the necessity of the planned aid' has not been challenged in the context of the appeal.
The Authority therefore considers that it is appropriate in this case to base the finding of incompatibility on a temporal lapse, namely that (i) the incentive effect test required by the Regional Aid Guidelines 2007 was not met and (ii) the work on the project had already started before the conclusion of the investment agreement and before the granting of the aid.
The Authority also rejects Becromal's argument that the Authority should examine the project in the light of the LIP Guidelines and apply the incentive effect test described therein.
The Authority notes that for certain large investment projects, the incentive effect test under point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an aid application has first been submitted and eligibility has been determined before work starts) is complemented by a second economic nature test (based on an economic analysis of the aid's contribution to a change in the behaviour of the aid beneficiary) under point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines and the LIP Guidelines.
The LIP guidelines provide for a detailed examination of certain large investment projects, i.e. those projects which have a significant impact on trade and which could lead to significant distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the LIP Guidelines and point 49 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment with eligible expenditure exceeding EUR 50 million.
As mentioned above, the Becromal investment agreement covers investment costs totalling EUR 117,25 million for both phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have provided the Authority with information regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs of the project.
Furthermore, none of these parties provided the Authority with information on the conditions of the detailed examination obligation under the LIP Guidelines, which are also referred to in point 57 (a) and (b) of the Regional Aid Guidelines 2007 (i.e. that the beneficiary is responsible for more than 25% of the sales of the product concerned on the market concerned and that the capacity created by the investment project is more than 5% of the market for the product concerned).
The Authority considers that there is no indication that the Becromal project exceeds the thresholds set out in point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis described in the LIP Guidelines is not applicable to the Becromal Investment Agreement.
Consequently, according to the case law in force and the wording of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, it is sufficient to establish whether the beneficiary has submitted an aid application; in addition, the competent authority must confirm the measure in writing before work begins, but the aid is conditional on the approval of the Authority.
According to the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, 'start of works' means 'the start of construction work or the first binding commitment to order equipment, excluding feasibility studies'.
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work on the Becromal project started in 2008 and thus before the signing of the previous agreement on 7 July 2009.
The Authority cannot accept the argument put forward by Becromal that the 'start of works' should be limited to the ordering of equipment specifically designed for the project in question and that the purchase of land and/or the construction of a factory facility is not necessarily an investment decision, as such an investment could easily be resold at any time.
This interpretation is supported by the case law of the Court of Justice, according to which even small investments by the beneficiary of the aid are sufficient to have legal effect and thus constitute the first stage of the work.
Moreover, the information available to the Authority indicates that Becromal started construction of the plant in early 2008 and that the recruitment process was launched later that year.

State aid within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
The Authority will examine below whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement are met for (i) the scheme as amended by the Supplementary Regulation and (ii) the five investment agreements.
In order to qualify as State aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of the application of the State aid rules, the term 'State' also covers regional and local entities.
A loss of tax revenue is equivalent to the consumption of State resources in the form of tax expenditure and public aid may be provided both through tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory or administrative nature and through the practices of the tax administration.
A reduction in the tax base or a reduction, total or partial, in the amount of tax, duty or charge implies a loss of revenue and therefore amounts to the consumption of State resources in the form of tax expenditure.

Measures favouring certain undertakings or certain products

The Commission notes that the aid granted to the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements is limited to the minimum necessary to ensure that the aid is limited to the minimum necessary to ensure the viability of the investment project.
The concept of aid is more general than that of subsidy, since it includes not only positive benefits, such as subsidies themselves, but also State interventions which, in various forms, lighten the burdens normally placed on the budget of an undertaking and which, therefore, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are of the same nature and have the same effects.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which public authorities grant certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although not involving a transfer of State resources, places the beneficiaries in a more favourable financial situation than other taxpayers constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allowed the beneficiaries to be relieved of part of the costs that they would normally have had to bear themselves in the course of their activity, such as the corporate tax applicable at a given time, the normal municipal property tax and the generally applicable social security contribution.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and certain production within the meaning of Article 61 (1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the Contracting Parties
It is settled case-law that, for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to establish that the aid has an actual effect on trade between the Contracting Parties and that there is an actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether the aid is liable to affect trade and distort competition.
Moreover, where aid granted by an EFTA State strengthens the position of an undertaking compared with other undertakings competing in intra-EEA trade, the latter must be regarded as affected by the aid.
The Authority had concluded in Decision No 390/10/COL that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the beneficiary undertakings in the eligible regions compared to that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The investment agreement with Becromal strengthens the competitive position of Becromal in relation to other producers of anodised aluminium foil for electrolytic capacitors.

According to Article 1 (c) of Part II of Protocol 3, 'any modification of existing aid' must be considered as new aid.
According to Article 4 (1) of the Authority's Decision No 195/04/COL on the implementing measures referred to in Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (the 'Implementing Decision'), '[...] modification of an existing aid means any change other than changes of a purely formal or administrative nature which are not such as to affect the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market'.
Therefore, the amendments which do not concern the substance of the aid do not affect the way in which the measure is to be classified.
The amendments made by the Supplementary Regulation provide that aid may be granted for projects whose work began before aid was granted - in fact, even before the scheme came into force.

Since, according to Article 1 (c) of Part II of Protocol 3, any modification of existing aid must be considered as new aid, the question arises whether the modified existing aid must be classified as new aid, or whether this is only the case for the modifications as such.

In order to be able to apply the State aid clauses provided for in Articles 61 to 63 of the EEA Agreement, State aid must be granted to undertakings related to the manufacture of products covered by the EEA Agreement.
Article 8 (3) of the EEA Agreement provides as follows:
products falling within Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System, with the exception of products listed in Protocol 2;
products covered by Protocol 3 and subject to the specific arrangements contained in that Protocol.
Article 8 (3) of the EEA Agreement therefore limits the material scope of the EEA Agreement to the above products, except where otherwise provided for in the Agreement.
Fish and fishery products, in so far as they do not fall within Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System and are not specified in Protocol 3, are outside the scope of the EEA Agreement.

In order to qualify as State aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State funds.
For the purposes of the application of the State aid rules, the term 'State' also covers regional and local authorities.
A loss of tax revenue is equivalent to the consumption of State funds in the form of tax expenditure, and State aid may also be granted through tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory or administrative nature, as well as through tax administration practices.
A reduction in the taxable base or a total or partial reduction in the amount of taxes, fees or charges implies a loss of tax revenue and therefore amounts to the consumption of State resources in the form of tax expenditure.

The Commission notes that the aid granted to the beneficiary of the investment agreement is limited to the minimum necessary to ensure the viability of the project.
The definition of aid is more general than that of a subsidy, since it includes not only positive benefits such as subsidies themselves, but also State interventions which, in various forms, alleviate the burdens normally borne by the budget of an undertaking and, therefore, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, have a similar character and produce the same effect.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which public authorities grant to certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although not involving a transfer of State funds, places those to whom the exemption applies in a more favourable financial situation than the rest of the taxpayers, constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.

Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the Contracting Parties
According to settled case-law, for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to prove the actual impact of the aid on trade between the Contracting Parties and the actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether such aid is liable to affect such trade and distort competition.
Moreover, where aid granted by an EFTA State serves to strengthen the position of an undertaking vis-à-vis other undertakings competing with it in intra-EEA trade, such trade should be considered to be affected by the aid.
The Authority concluded in Decision 390/10/COL that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions, compared to their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position vis-à-vis other producers of anodised aluminium foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As confirmed by Becromal, the market for anodised sheets used in aluminium electrolytic capacitors is global, and Becromal's customers include companies in [...] and [...].
Therefore, the fact that Becromal's main competitors are companies of [...] does not mean that there are no effects on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement strengthens Verne's competitive position vis-à-vis other data centres in Iceland.
In addition, Verne operates a global wholesale data centre, the services of which will be available to customers in the EEA and the global market.
The investment agreement strengthens the competitive position of Kísilfélagið in relation to other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica dust.
The market for metallurgical silicon and silica powder is worldwide.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens the competitive position of Thorsil in relation to the other producers of metallic silicon.
As stated in the preamble to the Thorsil investment agreement, the project was initiated, inter alia, to meet an increased demand for metallic silicon from Europe and North America.
The investment agreement with GMR Endurvinnslan strengthens the competitive position of GMR Endurvinnslan in relation to other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
The company intends to export most of its products to buyers in the construction sector.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions compared to their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.

In its opening decision, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements had been granted under the scheme, as the agreements appeared, at first sight, to have been affected by the changes to the scheme resulting from the Supplementary Regulation.
In accordance with Article 1 (c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid are to be considered as new aid.
According to Article 4 (1) of the Authority's Decision No 195/04/COL on the detailed rules for the implementation of Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (hereinafter referred to as the 'Implementing Decision'), '... modification of an existing aid means any change which does not constitute a change of a purely formal or administrative nature without any impact on the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market'.
Therefore, modifications which do not affect the substance of the aid do not affect the way in which the measure must be classified.
The amendments introduced by the Supplementary Regulation allow aid to be granted to projects on which work has started before the aid is granted, even before the scheme enters into force.
The amendments introduced by the Supplementary Regulation alter the basic features of the scheme.
In particular, the requirement of the incentive effect scheme has been removed.
This requirement was an important part of the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measures granted under the scheme as initially approved (as described in Decision No 390/10/COL).
The Authority therefore considers that the amendments to the scheme introduced by the Supplementary Regulation constitute a substantial modification of the scheme as approved.
Given that, under Article 1 (c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid are to be considered as new aid, the question arises whether the modified existing aid is to be considered as new aid, or only the modifications as such.

The Authority should therefore assess whether the amendments introduced by the Supplementary Regulation are separable from the initial scheme.
If this were the case, the modification constitutes new aid as such and must therefore be assessed in the light of Article 61 (3) (c) and the Authority's Guidelines on State aid for regional development for the period 2007-2013 (hereinafter 'the 2007 RAG').
According to settled case-law, a modification of an existing aid scheme extending it to a new category of beneficiaries can be clearly dissociated from the initial scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the initial scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3 (c) of the Incentives Regulation states that aid may also be granted to a project which has started after a special investment agreement has been concluded and before the entry into force of the Supplementary Regulation.
This amendment extended the scope of the scheme beyond that approved by the Authority's Decision No 390/10/COL, by allowing aid to be granted to projects on which work had started before the entry into force of the scheme, and which would therefore not fulfil the incentive effect criterion contained in the scheme as approved.

Moreover, the Authority considers that the amendments introduced by the Supplementary Regulation to Articles 8 and 20 of the Incentives Regulation, i.e. the reduction of the reference rate for corporate tax and the extension of the maximum duration of tax exemptions, were introduced only to benefit this new category of beneficiaries.
These new beneficiaries may therefore receive more aid than was authorised by the initially approved scheme.
These changes to the scheme are therefore fundamentally interrelated.

The objective of paragraph 30 of the 2007 RAG is to enable the Authority to determine whether the investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without undertaking a full analysis of the economic circumstances of the beneficiary's investment decision, at the time it was made.

Therefore, as confirmed by the General Court in Kronoply, the Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect on a chronological circumstance.
Becromal has argued that the assessment of State aid cases has changed with the introduction of a more detailed economic analysis, so that when assessing the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also assess its economic nature (i.e. whether it has contributed to a change in the behaviour of the aid beneficiary), also taking into account the provisions of the Regional Aid Guidelines for large investment projects.
The Authority cannot accept this argument.
Becromal refers to the judgment of the Court of Justice in HGA to argue that: [...] the Court, in its examination of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, states that the only factor determining whether the aid measure had an incentive effect is to examine whether the behaviour of the investor has changed, and did not take into account all the references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case.
However, Becromal's interpretation of the HGA judgment does not take into account the express statement of the Court of Justice that the General Court's finding that '[...] the priority of the aid application over the start of implementation of the investment project is a simple, relevant and appropriate criterion allowing the Commission to presume the necessity of the planned aid' was not challenged in the appeal procedure.
The Authority therefore considers that in this case its conclusion of incompatibility must be based on a chronological assessment: namely that (i) the incentive requirement laid down in the 2007 RAG was not fulfilled, and (ii) work on the project started before the investment agreement was concluded and the aid was granted.
The Authority also rejects Becromal's claim that the Authority should examine the project in the light of the Regional Aid Guidelines for Large Investment Projects and apply the incentive effect requirement laid down therein.
The Authority notes that for some large investment projects, the incentive effect test laid down in point 30 of the 2007 Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application has been submitted and eligibility confirmed before work begins) is complemented by a second test, of an economic nature (based on an economic analysis that the aid contributes to a change in the behaviour of the beneficiary of the aid), provided for in point 57 of the Regional Aid Guidelines and in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects.
The Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain large investment projects, i.e. those that have significant effects on trade and may lead to substantial distortions of competition.
According to paragraph 3 of the Regional Aid Guidelines for Large Investment Projects and paragraph 49 of the 2007 Guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment whose eligible costs exceed EUR 50 million.
As mentioned above, the Becromal investment agreement refers to EUR 117,25 million as the total investment cost for the two phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have submitted any information to the Authority regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs of the project.
Nor have they provided information on the conditions of the detailed verification obligation laid down in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects referred to in paragraph 57 (a) and (b) of the 2007 RAG (i.e. that the aid beneficiary has more than 25% of the products concerned on the relevant market, and that the production capacity created by the project represents more than 5% of the relevant product market).
In the Authority's view, there is no indication that the Becromal project exceeds the ceilings set out in paragraph 57 of the 2007 Guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis provided for in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects does not apply to the Becromal investment agreement.
Therefore, in the light of the case law and the wording of the 2007 RAG, it is sufficient to check whether the aid application has been submitted by the beneficiary and whether the granting authority has provided a written confirmation, conditional on the approval of the measure by the Authority, before work on the project starts.
The 2007 RAG define the term 'Start of works' as 'the start of construction works, or the first firm commitment for the order of equipment, excluding the preliminary feasibility studies'.
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work started on the Becromal project in 2008, even before the signing of the previous agreement of 7 July 2009.
The Authority cannot accept Becromal's argument that the 'start of works' should be limited to orders for equipment specifically designed for the project in question, and that the acquisition of land or the construction of a plant does not necessarily signal the beginning of an investment decision, as such an investment could easily be sold at a given time.
This interpretation is supported by the case law of the General Court, according to which even a small level of investment by the beneficiary is sufficient to produce legal effects and is therefore considered to constitute the first stage of the works.
Moreover, the information available to the Authority shows that Becromal started construction of the plant in early 2008, and its tendering procedure was launched at the end of that year.
