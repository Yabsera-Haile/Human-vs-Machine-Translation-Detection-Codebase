The Marmeti investment agreement concerned the construction and operation of a fish processing factory in Sandgerði.

For the state aid rules in Articles 61 to 63 of the EEA Agreement to be applicable, the state aid must be granted to undertakings that produce products falling within the scope of the EEA Agreement.
Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:

Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement thus limits the material scope of the EEA Agreement to the goods mentioned, unless otherwise provided in the Agreement.
Fish and fishery products, insofar as they do not fall under Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized System for the Description and Coding of Goods (HS) or are mentioned in Protocol 3 to the EEA Agreement, do not fall within the general scope of the Agreement.

State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
In the following, the supervisory authority will assess whether the criteria for the existence of state aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are met for i) the scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation and ii) the five investment agreements.
Classification as state aid requires that an advantage is granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of State aid rules, the term "State" covers both regional and local authorities.
A loss of tax revenue is a consumption of state resources as in the case of tax expenditures; state support can be granted not only through tax laws, regulations and administrative provisions, but also through the practices of the tax administration.
A reduction of the tax base or a complete or partial reduction of the tax amount, fees or levies implies a loss of revenue and is therefore equivalent to the use of state funds in the form of tax expenditures.
The regulation amended by the supplementary ordinance
In its Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme constitutes a transfer of state resources.
The changes associated with the supplementary regulation have no impact on this conclusion.
The five investment agreements included in particular the following exceptions for taxes and levies:
The maximum corporate tax rate for the company investing in the project in question was set at the tax rate applicable at the time of signing the agreement between the company and the Ministry of Industry.
Various exceptions were granted from the depreciation rules:
Instead of applying the statutory income tax rate of 10% according to Law No. 90/2003, assets are depreciated so that they have a calculated residual value of 0 at the end, and
The beneficiary may choose in the year in which the assets are put into use whether depreciation for these goods should be calculated on a pro-rata basis instead of annual depreciation, as required by Article 34 of Law No. 90/2003.
Exemptions were granted from the industrial levies established in Law No. 134/1992 and from the market levies established in Law No. 160/2002.
The stamp duty on documents related to the investment was set at 0.15% instead of the fee required under Law No. 36/1978.
The municipality's property tax was set at a rate 30% lower than the maximum rate required in Chapter II of Law No. 4/1995.
The general social insurance contributions were set at a rate 20% lower than required in Article 2, paragraph 3 of Law No. 113/1990.
Exemptions from customs duties were granted in accordance with Law No. 88/2005 as well as exemptions from excise tax in accordance with Law No. 97/1987.
Furthermore, the preamble of the Kísilfélagið investment agreement refers to a license and fee agreement between Kísilfélagið, the municipality of Reykjanesbær and the Harbour Fund Company in connection with the project, including principles on property tax and land lease.
The leasing of land or property below market value means lower tax revenues and thus represents a transfer of state funds.
In light of the above considerations, the Supervisory Authority concludes that state resources are involved in all five investment agreements.
Favoring of certain companies or production sectors
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, as only undertakings investing in certain areas in Iceland that are eligible under Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement are entitled to the corresponding aid.
The Surveillance Authority accepts the Icelandic authorities' reasoning that the changes to the scheme accompanying the supplementary regulation increase the benefits granted to Becromal under the scheme.
The amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation therefore constitute a selective measure, as they only affect a specific company.
Accordingly, the Surveillance Authority concludes that the supplementary regulation constitutes a favor to certain undertakings or production sectors within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
The aid recipients under the five investment agreements are also individual companies, therefore the measures are selective.
The concept of state aid is broader than the concept of subsidy, as it encompasses not only positive benefits such as subsidies themselves, but also state measures that reduce in various forms the burdens that an undertaking normally has to bear and which, although not subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are equivalent to them in nature and effect.
According to established case law, a measure by which state authorities grant certain undertakings an exemption from charges, which, although not involving a transfer of state resources, places the beneficiaries in a more favorable financial position than other taxpayers, constitutes state aid or aid granted through state resources.
The five investment agreements allow the beneficiaries to be exempted from part of the costs that they would normally have to bear themselves as part of their business activities, such as the payment of applicable corporate tax at a specific time, the statutory municipal property tax, and the generally applicable social security contributions.
The Surveillance Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favor certain undertakings and production sectors within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortions of competition and impairment of trade between the contracting parties
According to established case law, the classification of a national measure as state aid does not require proof of an actual effect of the aid on trade between Member States and an actual distortion of competition, but only an examination of whether the aid is liable to affect such trade or distort competition.
If aid granted by an EFTA Member State strengthens the position of an undertaking compared to other competitors in trade within the EEA, such trade must be regarded as being affected by the aid.
In its Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the supported undertakings in the eligible areas vis-à-vis actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position against other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
Becromal has confirmed that the market for anode foils in aluminum electrolytic capacitors is a global market and that Becromal's customers include companies from [...] and [...].
The fact that Becromal's largest competitors are companies from [...] does not mean, however, that the agreement has no effect on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement strengthens Verne's competitive position against other data centers in Iceland.
Furthermore, Verne operates a global data center for the wholesale market, whose services are available to customers both in the EEA and on the global market.
The Kísilfélagið investment agreement strengthens Kísilfélagið's competitive position against other manufacturers of metallurgical silicon and silicon dioxide dust.
The market for metallurgical silicon and for silicon dioxide dust is a global market.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens Thorsil's competitive position against other silicon metal manufacturers.
According to the preamble of this investment agreement, the project was initiated, among other things, to satisfy the increased demand for silicon metal in Europe and North America.
The GMR-Endurvinnslan investment agreement strengthens GMR Endurvinnslan's competitive position against other recycling companies and steel manufacturers.
The company intends to deliver the majority of its products to buyers in the construction industry.
Accordingly, the supervisory authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the respective supported companies in the eligible areas vis-à-vis their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
Based on the foregoing considerations, the Surveillance Authority concludes that the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation as well as the five investment agreements constitute State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
In its opening decision, the Surveillance Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements was granted in accordance with the scheme approved by the Surveillance Authority, as the agreements appeared prima facie to have been affected by amendments to the scheme made by the supplementary regulation.
According to Part II Article 1 letter c of Protocol 3, alterations to existing aid are to be regarded as new aid.
According to Article 4(1) of Decision No 195/04/COL of the Surveillance Authority on the implementing provisions of Part II Article 27 of Protocol 3 (the "Implementing Decision"), "[...] any change, other than modifications of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the evaluation of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market, shall be considered an alteration to existing aid".
Changes that do not affect the fundamental nature of the aid therefore do not influence the classification of the measure.
The amendments made by the supplementary regulation allow aid to be granted for projects that were already started before the aid was granted, even before the regulation came into force.
The changes made by the supplementary regulation alter the fundamental characteristics of the scheme.
In particular, the requirement of an incentive effect was removed from the regulation.
This requirement formed an essential component of the compatibility assessment for aid measures under the originally approved scheme (see description in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Accordingly, the Supervisory Authority takes the view that these amendments to the scheme made by the supplementary regulation constitute a fundamental change to the originally approved scheme.
In view of the fact that according to Part II Article 1 letter c of Protocol 3 alterations to existing aid are to be regarded as new aid, the question arises whether the altered existing aid in its entirety or only the alterations are to be classified as new aid.
In its judgment in the Gibraltar case, the Court of First Instance ruled:
Consequently, the supervisory authority must assess whether the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation can be separated from the original scheme.
If this is the case, then the changes constitute new aid and must therefore be reassessed against the background of Article 61(3)(c) and the Authority's Guidelines on State aid for regional purposes 2007-2013 (the "Regional Aid Guidelines 2007").
According to established case law, an amendment to an existing aid scheme that extends the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries is clearly separable from the original scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries has no impact on the assessment of the compatibility of the original scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3(c) of the Investment Incentives Regulation states that aid may also be granted for a project that was already started before the supplementary regulation entered into force, but after the conclusion of a special investment agreement.
This amendment expanded the scope of the scheme and went beyond the scope established in the Surveillance Authority's Decision No 390/10/COL, as aid can thus also be granted for projects that had already started before the scheme entered into force and would therefore not have passed the test regarding the incentive effect laid down in the approved scheme.
The supplementary regulation thus opened up the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries.
Furthermore, the supervisory authority is of the opinion that the amendments to Articles 8 and 20 of the Investment Incentive Regulation introduced by the supplementary regulation, i.e. the reduction of the corporate tax reference rate and the extension of the maximum duration of the tax exemption, were merely introduced so that this new category of beneficiaries can benefit from this scheme.
These new beneficiaries can thus receive more aid than was the case under the originally approved scheme.
For this reason, the changes to the regulation are fundamentally interconnected.
However, these changes do not affect the compatibility of individual measures that were already introduced under the originally approved scheme.
The supervisory authority therefore considers these changes associated with the supplementary regulation as changes that can be clearly separated from the original regulation.
Consequently, the supervisory authority takes the view that these amendments to the scheme constitute a new aid measure.
Furthermore, it follows that any aid granted on the basis of the supplementary regulation must also be regarded as new aid.
The supervisory authority is therefore concerned with assessing the compatibility of the amendments to the scheme through the supplementary regulation.

The supervisory authority is now dealing with the review of the five investment projects individually.
The Becromal investment agreement relates to an investment in a facility for anodizing aluminum foil, which was to be built in two phases in the city of Akureyri.
The first phase was to be completed by the end of March 2011, the second phase is to be finished by the end of 2014; the investment costs were estimated at a total of around 117.25 million EUR.
Sections D, H and I of the preamble of the Becromal Investment Agreement refer to an earlier investment agreement for the same project, which the same contracting parties entered into on July 7, 2009.
As previously discussed, the Icelandic authorities stated that the supplementary regulation was adopted with the aim of extending the applicable scheme to the Becromal project.
In their view, this amendment, which allowed the company Becromal to apply for investment incentives under this scheme, was necessary for the Icelandic authorities to fulfill their contractual obligations with Becromal.
The reference to a "special [...] investment agreement [...] before the law came into force" was therefore included in the Investment Incentive Regulation through the supplementary regulation, so that Becromal would also be covered by this provision.
Becromal could therefore have passed the incentive effect test required by the regulation solely on the basis of the changes introduced by the supplementary regulation.
The supervisory authority has already stated that the changes to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation constitute new aid that can be separated from the original scheme.
It follows that the aid granted under the Becromal investment agreement must be regarded as new aid, since the agreement was concluded against the background of the Investment Incentives Regulation as amended by the supplementary regulation.
The supervisory authority has already concluded that any aid granted on the basis of the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation must be regarded as new aid that is not compatible with the EEA Agreement.
It follows that the Becromal investment agreement constitutes incompatible aid, as it does not satisfy the incentive effect test under the approved scheme and thus does not fall within the scope of that scheme.
For the sake of completeness and to address the arguments put forward by Becromal and the Icelandic authorities, the Surveillance Authority has alternatively assessed the Becromal investment agreement as an ad hoc measure.
For this purpose, the supervisory authority refers to the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines and in particular to the requirement contained therein regarding the incentive effect.
According to point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, the presence of an incentive effect is an essential prerequisite for the compatibility of the aid:
The aim of the provisions in point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines is to enable the monitoring authority to verify whether investment aid creates a sufficient incentive effect without conducting a complete analysis of the economic preconditions at the time of the beneficiary's investment decision.
Should such a review not be guaranteed before work on the project begins, "then the entire project cannot receive any subsidies," and is therefore not eligible for funding.
As confirmed by the Court in its judgment in the Kronoply case, the supervisory authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect on a chronological sequence.
Becromal argued that the legal assessment of state aid cases had changed with the introduction of a more refined economic analysis and that the supervisory authority must therefore also assess the economic nature of the incentive effect (i.e., whether the aid contributed to a change in the beneficiary's behavior) and in doing so must also observe the provisions of the LIP Guidelines.
The supervisory authority cannot follow this argument.
Becromal refers to the Court's judgment in the HGA case to argue that: "[...] the Court, in its review of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, decided that the only decisive factor for determining whether the aid had an incentive effect was to establish whether it changed the behavior of the investors, and disregarded all references by the Court of First Instance to the Kronoply case".
Becromal, however, in its interpretation of the judgment in the HGA case, disregards the Court's express opinion that "[...] the requirement that the aid application must precede the start of implementation of the aid is a simple, appropriate and proportionate criterion which enables the Commission to presume the necessity of the planned aid" was not challenged in the appeal.
The Surveillance Authority therefore takes the view that in this case it is appropriate to base the finding of incompatibility on a temporal sequence, namely that i) the examination of the incentive effect prescribed in the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines was not fulfilled and ii) work on the project had already commenced before the conclusion of the investment agreement and before the aid was granted.
The Monitoring Authority also rejects Becromal's argument that the Monitoring Authority must examine the project against the background of the LIP guidelines and apply the incentive effect test described therein.
The supervisory authority states that for certain large investment projects, the examination of the incentive effect pursuant to point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an aid application must first be submitted and eligibility established before work begins) is supplemented by a second examination of an economic nature (based on an economic analysis regarding the contribution of the aid to a change in the beneficiary's behavior) pursuant to point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines and the LIP Guidelines.
The LIP guidelines provide for an in-depth examination of certain large investment projects, i.e. those projects that have significant effects on trade and could lead to substantial distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the LIP guidelines and point 49 of the 2007 regional aid guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment with eligible expenditure exceeding EUR 50 million.
As already mentioned above, the Becromal investment agreement covers investment costs totaling EUR 117.25 million for both phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have provided the Surveillance Authority with information regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs for the project.
Furthermore, none of these parties has provided the supervisory authority with information on the conditions for the detailed assessment obligation pursuant to the LIP guidelines, to which point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines also refer (i.e., that the aid beneficiary is responsible for more than 25% of sales of the product concerned in the relevant market and that the capacity created by the investment project amounts to more than 5% of the market for the product concerned).
The Surveillance Authority takes the view that there is no indication that the Becromal project exceeds the limits set out in point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines.
The supervisory authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis described in the LIP guidelines is not applicable to the Becromal investment agreement.
Consequently, according to applicable case law and according to the wording of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, it is sufficient to establish whether the aid recipient has submitted an aid application; furthermore, the competent authority must confirm the measure in writing before work begins, but the aid is subject to approval by the monitoring authority.
According to the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, "start of works" means "either the start of construction works or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding feasibility studies".
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work on the Becromal project began in 2008, thus before the signing of the earlier agreement on 7 July 2009.
The supervisory authority cannot follow Becromal's argument that the "start of works" should be limited to the ordering of equipment specifically manufactured for the project in question, and that the acquisition of land and/or the construction of a factory facility does not necessarily constitute an investment decision, as such an investment could easily be resold at any time.
This interpretation is supported by the Court's case law, according to which even small investments by the aid recipient are sufficient to produce legal effects; they thus constitute the first phase of the works.
Furthermore, the information available to the supervisory authority indicates that Becromal began construction of the facility in early 2008 and that the recruitment process was launched later that year.
Similarly, they noted that the supplementary regulation had no impact on the Verne investment agreement since no investment agreement had been previously concluded with Verne.

This is the reason why the Icelandic authorities maintained that there had been no investment agreement previously concluded with Verne that could constitute a "special investment agreement" within the meaning of the supplementary regulation.
However, the Authority also noted that it did not have sufficient information at the time regarding the status of this project.

The construction work on the Becromal project began following the investment agreement concluded on May 29, 2009, which was prior to the conclusion of the Becromal investment agreement on December 30, 2010,

The investment agreement concluded with Marmeti concerned the construction and operation of a fish processing plant in Sandgerði.
Marmeti's activities included salting and drying fish and seafood.
For the State aid provisions of Articles 61 to 63 of the EEA Agreement to apply, aid must be granted to undertakings active in the production of products covered by the EEA Agreement.
However, Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
to products falling within Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized System for the designation and codification of goods, excluding the products listed in Protocol 2;
to the products listed in Protocol 3, subject to the specific procedures provided for therein."
Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement therefore limits the material scope of the latter to the aforementioned products, unless otherwise provided in the EEA Agreement.
Fish and fishery products, insofar as they do not fall under chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) and are not listed in Protocol 3, do not fall within the general scope of the EEA Agreement.
Although Article 4 of Protocol 9 of the EEA Agreement provides that "aid granted through State resources to the fisheries sector which distorts competition shall be abolished", the competence to assess State aid to the fisheries sector lies with the Contracting Parties to the EEA Agreement.
The Authority understands that the products manufactured by Marmeti fell under chapters 03, 05, 15, 16 and 23 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) and were therefore not covered by the EEA Agreement.
It therefore lacks jurisdiction to assess the aid potentially granted under the investment agreement concluded with Marmeti.
In light of the foregoing, the Authority closes its examination of the investment agreement concluded with Marmeti.

State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
The Authority will examine below whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are met for (i) the scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation and (ii) the five investment agreements.
To be classified as State aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of applying State aid rules, the term "State" also covers regional and local entities.
A loss of tax revenue is equivalent to the consumption of State resources in the form of tax expenditures and public aid can be provided through tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory or administrative nature as well as through tax administration practices.
A reduction in the tax base or a reduction, total or partial, in the amount of tax, duties or charges, implies a loss of revenue and therefore equates to the consumption of State resources in the form of tax expenditures.
The scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation
The Authority had concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme constituted a transfer of State resources.
The amendments made by the supplementary regulation do not modify this conclusion.
In particular, the five investment agreements contained the following exemptions regarding taxes and charges:
the maximum corporate tax rate for the company that would invest in the project concerned was set at the rate in force on the date of signature of an agreement between the company and the Ministry of Industry;
derogations were granted to the rules relating to depreciation:
the depreciation of assets to a zero residual value, instead of the 10% tax provided for by Law No. 90/2003; and
The beneficiary may, during the year in which the assets are put into service, choose to depreciate them in proportion to the annual depreciation, instead of a full year of depreciation, as required by Article 34 of Law No. 90/2003;
exemptions were granted to industrial charges listed in Law No. 134/1992 and to market charges provided for by Law No. 160/2002;
the stamp duty on investment-related documents was set at 0.15% instead of the legal stamp duty provided for by Law No. 36/1978;
the municipal property tax was set at a rate 30% lower than the maximum rate provided for in Chapter II of Law No. 4/1995;
the general social security contribution was set at a rate 20% lower than the rate provided for in Article 2, paragraph 3, of Law No. 113/1990;
Exemptions were granted from customs duties within the meaning of Law No. 88/2005 and from excise duties within the meaning of Law No. 97/1987.
Furthermore, the preamble of the investment agreement concluded with Kísilfélagið mentions an agreement between Kísilfélagið, the municipality of Reykjanesbær and the port investment company concerning the granting of licenses and charges related to the project, including principles regarding property tax and land rental.
Now, renting land or real estate at a price below market value implies a loss of revenue and therefore constitutes a transfer of State resources.
Given the above, the Authority concludes that State resources are also involved in the five investment agreements.
Measures favoring certain enterprises or certain productions
The Authority concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme was selective because only undertakings investing in certain regions of Iceland eligible for aid under Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement could benefit from aid under this scheme.
The Authority accepts the Icelandic authorities' argument that the amendments made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation extend to Becromal the benefits granted under the scheme.
These modifications therefore constitute a selective measure, given that they concern a specific company.
The Authority concludes, consequently, that the supplementary regulation favors certain undertakings and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Similarly, since the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are specific companies, the measures are selective.
The concept of aid is broader than that of subsidy, given that it includes not only positive benefits, such as subsidies themselves, but also State interventions which, in various forms, alleviate the charges that normally burden a company's budget and which, thereby, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are of the same nature and have identical effects.
According to established case law, a measure by which public authorities grant certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although not involving a transfer of State resources, places the beneficiaries in a more favorable financial situation than other taxpayers constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allowed the beneficiaries to be relieved of part of the costs they would normally have had to bear themselves as part of their business, such as the corporate tax applicable at a given time, the normal municipal property tax and the generally applicable social security contribution.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favor certain undertakings and certain production within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the contracting parties
It is settled case-law that for the purpose of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to establish an actual effect of the aid on trade between the contracting parties and an actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether the aid is liable to affect such trade and to distort competition.
Furthermore, when aid granted by an EFTA State strengthens the position of an undertaking compared with other competing undertakings in intra-EEA trade, the latter must be regarded as affected by the aid.
The Authority had concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of beneficiary undertakings in the eligible regions compared to that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The investment agreement concluded with Becromal strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As confirmed by Becromal, the market for anode foils used in aluminum electrolytic capacitors is a global market and Becromal's customers include companies [...] and [...].
Also, the fact that Becromal's main competitors are companies [...] does not mean that there is no effect on trade within the EEA.
The investment agreement concluded with Verne strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other data centers established in Iceland.
Furthermore, Verne operates a global wholesale data center whose services will be made available to customers within the EEA and on the global market.
The investment agreement concluded with Kísilfélagið strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica fume.
The market for metallurgical silicon and silica dust is global.
The investment agreement concluded with Thorsil strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other silicon metal producers.
As indicated in the preamble of said investment agreement, the project was notably launched to respond to the increase in demand for silicon metal in Europe and North America.
The investment agreement concluded with GMR Endurvinnslan strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
This company is considering exporting the majority of its products to sell them to buyers in the construction sector.
Consequently, the Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions compared to that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
Given the above, the Authority concludes that the modifications made by the supplementary regulation and the five investment agreements constitute State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
In its decision to open the procedure, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements fell within the scope of the scheme or not, since the agreements appeared, at first glance, to have been affected by the modifications made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation.
According to Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, "any alteration to existing aid" must be considered as new aid.
Under Article 4(1) of Authority Decision No 195/04/COL on implementing measures referred to in Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (the 'implementing decision'), '[...] alteration of existing aid means any change other than modifications of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the evaluation of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market'.
Therefore, modifications that do not concern the substance of the aid do not affect how the measure should be classified.
The amendments made by the supplementary regulation provide that aid may be granted for projects whose work began before the granting of aid — in fact, even before the scheme came into force.
The changes made by the supplementary regulation modify the fundamental characteristics of the scheme.
In particular, the requirement for an incentive effect provided for by the scheme has been removed.
However, this requirement constituted an essential element in assessing the compatibility of the aid measures granted under the scheme as initially authorized (Decision No 390/10/COL).
Consequently, the Authority considers that the modifications made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation constitute a substantial modification of the scheme as authorized.
Given that, according to Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, any alteration to existing aid must be considered as new aid, the question arises as to whether the altered existing aid should be classified as new aid, or whether this should only be the case for the alterations as such.
In the judgment it rendered in the Gibraltar case, the Court of First Instance stated the following:
Therefore, the Authority must examine whether the modifications made by the supplementary regulation are separable from the initial scheme.
If this is the case, the modifications constitute in themselves new aid and, therefore, must be assessed in the light of Article 61(3)(c) and the Authority's guidelines on regional State aid for the period 2007-2013 (the '2007 guidelines').
It is established case law that the modification of an existing aid scheme providing for its extension to a new category of beneficiaries is a modification that is clearly severable from the initial scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the initial scheme.
The new wording of Article 3(c) of the Regulation on incentive measures provides that aid may also be granted to a project that began after the conclusion of a special investment agreement and before the entry into force of the supplementary regulation.
This amendment broadened the scope of this scheme beyond what had been authorized by the Authority's Decision No 390/10/COL, insofar as it allowed the granting of aid to projects whose work had begun before the scheme entered into force and which, therefore, would not have satisfied the incentive effect criterion set out in the scheme as authorized.
The supplementary regulation has therefore opened the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries.
The Authority further considers that the amendments made by the supplementary regulation to Articles 8 and 20 of the regulation on incentive measures, namely the reduction of the corporate tax reference rate and the extension of the maximum duration of tax exemptions, were added solely for the purpose of benefiting this new category of beneficiaries.
These new beneficiaries can therefore receive more substantial aid than what was permitted under the scheme as authorized.
Therefore, the changes made to the system are fundamentally linked.
They do not, however, affect the compatibility of the various measures already covered by the system as authorized.
The Authority therefore considers that the modifications made by the supplementary regulation constitute a modification that is clearly separable from the initial scheme.
It considers, therefore, that these modifications constitute new aid.
It follows, moreover, that any aid granted on the basis of the supplementary regulation will also be considered as new aid.
The Authority therefore focuses on examining the compatibility of the modifications made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation.

For the sake of completeness and in order to respond to the arguments put forward by Becromal and by the Icelandic authorities, the Authority has assessed, on a subsidiary basis, the investment agreement concluded with Becromal as an ad hoc measure.
To this end, the Authority refers to the 2007 guidelines and, in particular, to the incentive effect requirement contained therein.
In accordance with point 30 of the 2007 guidelines, the existence of an incentive effect is a necessary precondition for compatibility:
Point 30 of the 2007 guidelines aims to enable the Authority to verify whether investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without conducting a complete examination of all economic aspects of the aid beneficiary's investment decision at the time it was made.
If these assurances are not given and work begins, "the entire project loses its right to assistance," meaning that the project as such is not eligible.
Thus, as confirmed by the Court in the Kronoply judgment, the Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect by reference to a chronological circumstance.
Becromal argued that the assessment of State aid cases has evolved, with the introduction of more refined economic analysis, so that when assessing the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also evaluate its economic nature (i.e., verify whether it contributed to a change in the aid beneficiary's behavior), also taking into account the provisions of the guidelines on large investment projects.
The Authority cannot accept this argument.
Becromal invokes the Court of Justice judgment in the HGA case to argue that: "[...] the Court, in its review of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, held that the only determining factor as to whether the aid measure had an incentive effect was to examine whether it had changed the investor's behavior and dismissed all references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case".
However, Becromal's interpretation of the HGA judgment does not take into account the fact that the Court of Justice expressly found that the General Court's conclusion that "[...] the fact that the application for aid precedes the start of implementation of the investment project constitutes a simple, relevant and appropriate criterion enabling the Commission to presume that the planned aid is necessary" was not challenged in the appeal.
The Authority therefore considers that it is appropriate in this case to base its finding of incompatibility on a chronological assessment: more specifically, i) the incentive effect criterion set out in the 2007 guidelines was not met and ii) work began on the project before the conclusion of the investment agreement and the granting of the aid.
The Authority also rejects Becromal's argument that the Authority must examine the project in light of the guidelines on large investment projects and apply the incentive effect criterion prescribed therein.
The Authority notes that, for certain large investment projects, the incentive effect criterion provided for in point 30 of the 2007 guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application has been submitted and eligibility confirmed before work begins) is supplemented by a second criterion, of an economic nature (based on an economic analysis of whether the aid contributes to a change in the beneficiary's behavior), provided for in point 57 of the regional aid guidelines and in the guidelines on large investment projects.
The guidelines on major investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain major investment projects, namely those that have substantial effects on trade and may lead to significant distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the guidelines on large investment projects and point 49 of the 2007 guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment whose eligible expenditure exceeds EUR 50,000,000.
As indicated previously, the investment agreement concluded with Becromal mentions an amount of EUR 117,250,000 as the total investment cost for both phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities provided information to the Authority regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs related to the project.
Similarly, they have not provided any information on the conditions of the detailed verification obligation set out in the guidelines on large investment projects, to which point 57, (a) and (b), of the 2007 guidelines refers (namely that the aid beneficiary achieves more than 25% of sales of the products concerned on the relevant market and that the capacity created by the project represents more than 5% of the market for the product concerned).
According to the Authority, there is no indication that the Becromal project exceeds the thresholds set out in point 57 of the 2007 guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis provided for in the guidelines on major investment projects does not apply to the investment agreement concluded with Becromal.
Therefore, in light of the case law and the wording of the 2007 guidelines, it is sufficient to verify whether an aid application was submitted by the beneficiary and whether written confirmation from the granting authority was given subject to the measure being authorized by the Authority, before work on the project began.
According to the 2007 guidelines, the expression "start of works" means "either the start of construction works or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding preliminary feasibility studies".
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work had begun on the Becromal project in 2008, even before the signing of the previous agreement of July 7, 2009.
The Authority cannot accept Becromal's arguments that the "start of work" should be limited to ordering equipment specifically designed for the project in question and that the purchase of land and/or construction of a facility would not necessarily mark the beginning of an investment decision, as such investments could easily be sold at any time.
This interpretation is supported by the Court's case law, according to which even a low level of investment by the beneficiary is sufficient to produce legal effects and is therefore considered to constitute the first stage of the works.
Furthermore, the information available to the Authority shows that Becromal began construction work on its facility in early 2008 and that its recruitment process started later that same year.

The Marmeti investment agreement concerned the construction and operation of a fish processing plant in Sandgerði.
Marmeti's operations included the salting and drying of seafood products.
In order to apply the State aid provisions laid down in Articles 61 to 63 of the EEA Agreement, State aid must be granted to undertakings engaged in the manufacture of products covered by the EEA Agreement.
Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement provides as follows:
products included in Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System, except for the products listed in Protocol 2;
the products included in Protocol 3 and subject to the specific arrangements contained in that Protocol".
Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement therefore limits the material scope of application of the EEA Agreement to the products mentioned above, except where the Agreement provides otherwise.
Fish and fishery products, insofar as they do not fall within chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized System of designation and codification of goods and are not specified in Protocol 3, are outside the scope of the EEA Agreement.
Although Article 4 of Protocol 9 of the EEA Agreement establishes that "Aid granted to the fisheries sector from State resources shall be abolished when it distorts competition", the competence to assess State aid to the fisheries sector lies with the Contracting Parties to the EEA Agreement.
In the opinion of the Body, the products manufactured by Marmeti fell within chapters 03, 05, 15, 16 and 23 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) and were therefore outside the scope of the products covered by the EEA Agreement.
Therefore, the Authority has no competence to assess the possible aid granted under the provisions of the Marmeti investment agreement.
In light of the foregoing, the Body concludes its investigation into the Marmeti investment agreement.

Next, the Authority will assess whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are met for i) the scheme as amended by the Supplementary Regulation, and ii) the five investment agreements.
For a measure to qualify as state aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of applying state aid rules, the term "State" also covers regional and local authorities.
A loss of tax revenue is equivalent to the consumption of state funds in the form of tax expenditures, and state aid may also be granted through tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory or administrative nature, as well as through tax administration practices.
A reduction in the tax base or a total or partial reduction in the amount of taxes, fees or charges implies a loss of tax revenue and therefore amounts to the consumption of state resources in the form of tax expenditures.

Favoring certain companies or productions
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, in the sense that only companies investing in certain regions of Iceland and which can benefit from aid under Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement could receive aid under it.
The Authority accepts the Icelandic authorities' argument that the modifications to the scheme resulting from the Supplementary Regulation expanded the advantages granted to Becromal under the scheme.
Therefore, such modifications constitute a selective measure, as they affect a specific company.
The Authority therefore concludes that the Supplementary Regulation favors certain undertakings and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Similarly, the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are individual companies and, therefore, the measures are selective.
The definition of aid is more general than that of subsidy, as it comprises not only positive benefits, such as subsidies proper, but also state interventions that, in various forms, alleviate the burdens that normally fall on a company's budget and, therefore, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, have a similar character and produce the same effect.
According to established case law, a measure whereby public authorities grant certain companies a tax exemption which, although it does not involve a transfer of state funds, places those to whom the exemption applies in a more favorable financial situation than other taxpayers, constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allowed the beneficiaries to free themselves from part of the costs they would normally have had to bear in their business activity, such as the payment of corporate tax at the applicable rate at a given time, municipal property tax and the generally applicable social security contribution.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favor certain undertakings and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the Contracting Parties
According to established case law, for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to demonstrate the actual impact of the aid on trade between the contracting Member Parties and the actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether such aid is liable to affect such trade and distort competition.
Moreover, when aid granted by an EFTA State serves to strengthen the position of an undertaking compared with other undertakings competing in intra-EEA trade, such trade must be regarded as affected by the aid.
The Authority concluded in Decision 390/10/COL that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the aid beneficiary companies in the eligible regions, compared to their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position with respect to other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As Becromal has confirmed, the market for anodized foils used in aluminum electrolytic capacitors is global, and among Becromal's customers there are companies in […] and […].
Thus, the fact that Becromal's main competitors are [...] companies does not mean that there are no effects on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement strengthens Verne's competitive position relative to other data centers in Iceland.
Additionally, Verne operates a global wholesale data center whose services will be available to customers from the EEA and the global market.
The Kísilfélagið investment agreement strengthens Kísilfélagið's competitive position with respect to other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica powder.
The market for metallurgical silicon and silica powder is global.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens Thorsil's competitive position with respect to other metallic silicon producers.
As stated in the preamble of the Thorsil investment agreement, the project was initiated, among other things, to address an increase in demand for metallic silicon from Europe and North America.
The GMR Endurvinnslan investment agreement strengthens GMR Endurvinnslan's competitive position with respect to other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
The company intends to export most of its products to buyers in the construction sector.
Consequently, the Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions compared to their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
On the basis of the above, the Authority concludes that the modifications made by the Supplementary Regulation and the five investment agreements constitute State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
In its Opening Decision, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements had been granted within the scope of the scheme, since the agreements appeared, at first sight, to have been affected by the modifications to the scheme resulting from the Supplementary Regulation.
In accordance with Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid must be considered as new aid.
In accordance with Article 4(1) of Authority Decision No 195/04/COL on the implementing provisions of Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (hereinafter referred to as the 'Implementing Decision'), '[…] alteration of existing aid shall mean any change, other than modifications of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the evaluation of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market'.
Therefore, modifications that do not affect the essence of the aid do not affect how the measure should be classified.
The amendments introduced by the Supplementary Regulation allow aid to be granted to projects where work has begun before the aid is granted, even before the scheme enters into force.
The modifications introduced by the supplementary Regulation alter the basic characteristics of the regime.
In particular, the incentive effect scheme requirement has been removed.
This requirement was an important part of the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measures granted under the scheme, as initially approved (as described in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Consequently, the Body considers that the modifications to the regime introduced by the Supplementary Regulation constitute a substantial modification of the Regime as it was approved.
Considering that, under Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid must be considered new aid, the question arises whether the modified existing aid should be considered new aid, or only the modifications as such.

If this were the case, the modification constitutes, as such, new aid and, therefore, must be assessed in light of Article 61(3)(c) and the Authority's Guidelines on regional State aid for 2007-2013 (hereinafter, 'the 2007 RAG').
According to established case law, a modification of an existing aid scheme that extends it to a new category of beneficiaries can be clearly separated from the initial scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the initial scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3(c) of the Regulation on incentives specifies that aid may also be granted to a project that has begun after the conclusion of a special investment agreement and before the entry into force of the supplementary Regulation.
This modification expanded the scope of the scheme beyond what was approved by Body Decision No 390/10/COL, by allowing aid to be granted to projects where work had begun before the scheme entered into force, and which therefore would not meet the incentive effect criterion contained in the scheme as approved.
The supplementary Regulation, therefore, opened the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries.
On the other hand, the Authority considers that the modifications introduced by the Supplementary Regulation to Articles 8 and 20 of the Regulation on incentives, namely the reduction of the reference rate for corporate tax and the extension of the maximum duration of tax exemptions, were introduced solely to benefit this new category of beneficiaries.
These new beneficiaries may therefore receive more aid than that authorized by the initially approved scheme.
Therefore, these modifications to the regime are fundamentally interrelated.
However, these modifications do not affect the compatibility of the various measures already covered by the approved scheme.
Therefore, the Body considers that the modifications introduced by the supplementary Regulation constitute an alteration that can be clearly dissociated from the initial regime.
Consequently, the Body considers that these modifications to the regime constitute a new aid measure.
Furthermore, it follows from this that aid granted on the basis of the Supplementary Regulation will also be considered new aid.

For the sake of exhaustiveness and in order to respond to the allegations made by Becromal and the Icelandic authorities, the Authority has examined on a subsidiary basis the Becromal investment agreement as an ad hoc measure.
In this regard, the Authority refers to the 2007 RAG and, in particular, to the incentive effect requirement contained therein.
In accordance with paragraph 30 of the 2007 RAG, the existence of an incentive effect is a necessary precondition for compatibility:
The objective of paragraph 30 of the 2007 RAG is to allow the Authority to determine whether investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without conducting a complete analysis of the economic circumstances of the beneficiary's investment decision at the time it was made.
If these guarantees are not provided and work begins, "the entire project will not be eligible for aid," meaning the project as such is not eligible for funding.
Therefore, as confirmed by the General Court in the Kronoply judgment, the Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect on reference to a circumstance of a chronological nature.
Becromal has argued that the assessment of state aid matters has changed with the introduction of more detailed economic analysis, so that when evaluating the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also assess its economic nature (i.e., whether it has contributed to a change in the behavior of the aid beneficiary), also taking into account the provisions of the Regional Aid Guidelines for large investment projects.
The Board cannot accept this argument.
Becromal refers to the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: "[...] the Court, in its examination of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, establishes that the only factor determining whether the aid measure had an incentive effect is to examine whether it changed the investor's behavior, and did not take into account all the references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case."
However, Becromal's interpretation of the HGA judgment does not take into account the Court of Justice's express statement that the General Court's assessment that "[...] the fact that the aid application precedes the start of implementation of the investment project is a simple, relevant and appropriate criterion that allows the Commission to presume the necessary nature of the planned aid" was not called into question in the appeal proceedings.
Therefore, the Body considers that in this case it is necessary to base its conclusion of incompatibility on a chronological assessment: namely, that i) the incentive requirement established in the 2007 RAG was not met, and ii) work on the project began before the conclusion of the investment agreement and the granting of the aid.
The Authority also rejects Becromal's allegation that the Authority must examine the project in light of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects, and apply the incentive effect requirement established therein.
The Authority notes that, for some large investment projects, the incentive effect test established in point 30 of the 2007 RAG (based on the chronological requirement that an application has been submitted and eligibility has been confirmed before work begins) is complemented by a second test, of an economic nature (based on an economic analysis that the aid contributes to a change in the behavior of the aid beneficiary), provided for in paragraph 57 of the Regional Aid Guidelines and in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects.
The Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain large investment projects, namely those that have significant effects on trade and may lead to substantial distortions of competition.
According to paragraph 3 of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects and paragraph 49 of the 2007 RAG, a large investment project means an initial investment with eligible expenditure exceeding EUR 50 million.
As mentioned above, the Becromal investment agreement refers to EUR 117.25 million as the total investment cost for the two phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have submitted any information to the Authority regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs of the project.
Nor have they provided information on the conditions for the detailed verification obligation established in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects referred to in paragraph 57, letters a) and b), of the 2007 RAG (i.e., that the aid beneficiary has more than 25% of the products in question in the reference market, and that the production capacity created by the project represents more than 5% of the reference product market).
In the Authority's opinion, there is nothing to indicate that the Becromal project exceeds the maximum limits provided for in paragraph 57 of the 2007 Guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis provided for in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects does not apply to the Becromal investment agreement.
Therefore, in light of the case law and the wording of the 2007 RAG, it is sufficient to verify whether the aid application has been submitted by the beneficiary and whether the granting authority has provided written confirmation, conditional upon the measure's approval by the Authority, before work on the project begins.
The 2007 RAG define the term "start of works" as "the start of construction works or the first firm commitment for ordering equipment, excluding preliminary feasibility studies".
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work began on the Becromal project in 2008, even before the signing of the previous agreement of July 7, 2009.
The Board cannot accept Becromal's thesis that the "commencement of work" must be limited to orders for equipment specially designed for the project in question, and that the acquisition of land or the construction of a plant does not necessarily signal the beginning of an investment decision, since such investment could easily be sold at any given time.
This interpretation is corroborated by the case law of the General Court, according to which even a reduced level of investment by the beneficiary is sufficient to produce legal effects, and is therefore considered to constitute the first phase of the works.
On the other hand, the information available to the Board shows that Becromal began construction of the plant in early 2008, and its procurement procedure was initiated at the end of that year.
