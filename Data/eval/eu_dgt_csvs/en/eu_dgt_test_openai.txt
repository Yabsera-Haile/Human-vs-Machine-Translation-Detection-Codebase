The Marmeti investment agreement concerned the construction and operation of a fish-processing plant in Sandgerði.

In order for the rules on state aid in Articles 61 to 63 of the EEA Agreement to be applicable, the state aid must be granted to undertakings that manufacture products falling within the scope of the EEA Agreement.
Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
Goods falling under Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System, with the exception of the goods listed in Protocol 2;
Goods listed in Protocol 3, subject to the special provisions laid down therein.
Article 8(3) of the EEA Agreement thus limits the material scope of the EEA Agreement to the goods referred to, unless otherwise provided for in the Agreement.
Fish and fishery products do not fall within the general scope of the Agreement, insofar as they are neither covered by Chapters 25 to 97 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) nor mentioned in Protocol 3 to the EEA Agreement.
Article 4 of Protocol 9 to the EEA Agreement does indeed provide as follows: "Competition-distorting state aid in the fisheries sector shall be abolished"; however, the authority to assess state aid for the fisheries sector lies with the Contracting Parties to the EEA Agreement.
The Surveillance Authority takes the view that the products manufactured by Marmeti fall under Chapters 3, 5, 15, 16 and 23 of the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) and thus do not fall within the scope of the EEA Agreement.
The Surveillance Authority therefore does not have the power to assess potential State aid under the Marmeti investment agreement.

State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
In the following, the Surveillance Authority will assess whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are fulfilled for (i) the scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation and (ii) the five investment agreements.
The classification as State aid requires that an advantage be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of the state aid rules, the term "State" covers both regional and local bodies.
A loss of tax revenue is a consumption of public funds, as in the case of tax expenditures; government support may be provided not only through tax laws, regulations and administrative directives, but also through the practice of the tax administration.
A reduction in the tax base or a full or partial reduction in the amount of tax, fees, or charges entails a loss of revenue and is therefore equivalent to the use of state resources in the form of tax expenditures.

In its Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme constitutes a transfer of State resources.
The changes accompanying the supplementary regulation have no impact on this conclusion.

Favouring certain undertakings or branches of production
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, since only undertakings that invest in certain areas in Iceland that are eligible under Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement are entitled to the relevant aid.
The Surveillance Authority accepts the reasoning of the Icelandic authorities that the changes to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation increase the advantages conferred on Becromal by the scheme.
The changes introduced by the supplementary regulation therefore constitute a selective measure, since they affect only a specific undertaking.
Accordingly, the Surveillance Authority concludes that the supplementary regulation constitutes a favouring of certain undertakings or branches of production under Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
The aid beneficiaries under the five investment agreements are also individual undertakings; the measures are therefore selective.
The concept of State aid is broader than the concept of a subsidy, since it encompasses not only positive benefits such as subsidies themselves, but also government measures that, in various forms, reduce the burdens an undertaking normally has to bear and which, although not subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are equivalent to them in nature and effect.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which public authorities grant certain undertakings an exemption from charges, which does not, however, involve the transfer of State resources but places the recipients of the aid in a more favourable financial position than other taxpayers, constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements enable the beneficiaries to be exempted from part of the costs that they would normally have to bear in the course of their business activities, such as the payment of the applicable corporate income tax at a specific point in time, the municipality’s statutory property tax, and the generally applicable social security contributions.
The Surveillance Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and branches of production within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortions of competition and impairment of trade between the Contracting Parties
According to settled case-law, for a national measure to be classified as State aid, it is not necessary to demonstrate an actual effect of the aid on trade between Member States and an actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether the aid is liable to affect that trade or to distort competition.
If aid granted by an EFTA Member State strengthens the position of an undertaking vis-à-vis its competitors in trade within the EEA, that trade must be regarded as affected by the aid.
In its Decision No 390/10/COL, the Surveillance Authority concluded that the scheme had strengthened the competitive position of the aided undertakings in the eligible areas vis-à-vis actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal’s competitive position compared with other producers of anodised aluminium foil for electrolytic capacitors.
Becromal has confirmed that the market for anode foils in aluminium electrolytic capacitors is a global market and that Becromal’s customers include companies from […] and […].
However, the fact that Becromal's largest competitors are companies from […] does not mean that the agreement has no effect on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement strengthens Verne's competitive position compared to other data centers in Iceland.
In addition, Verne operates a global data center for the wholesale market, whose services are available to customers in the EEA as well as on the world market.
The Kísilfélagið investment agreement strengthens Kísilfélagið’s competitive position relative to other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica fume.
The market for metallurgical silicon and for silica fume is a global market.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens Thorsil’s competitive position relative to other silicon metal producers.
According to the preamble to this investment agreement, the project was initiated, among other things, to meet the increased demand for silicon metal in Europe and North America.
The GMR Endurvinnslan investment agreement strengthens GMR Endurvinnslan’s competitive position relative to other recycling companies and steel manufacturers.
The company intends to deliver most of its products to buyers in the construction industry.
Accordingly, the Surveillance Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the respective supported companies in the eligible areas vis-à-vis their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
In the light of the foregoing considerations, the Surveillance Authority concludes that the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation, as well as the five investment agreements, constitute State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
In its opening decision, the Surveillance Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements had been awarded in accordance with the scheme approved by the Surveillance Authority, since the agreements appeared, on the face of it, to have been affected by amendments to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation.
Pursuant to Part II, Article 1(c), of Protocol 3, alterations to existing aid shall be regarded as new aid.
According to Article 4(1) of Decision No 195/04/COL of the Surveillance Authority on the implementing provisions of Part II, Article 27 of Protocol 3 (the “Implementing Decision”), “[…] any change, other than a change of a purely formal or administrative nature which cannot affect the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market, shall be considered an alteration to existing aid”.
Changes that do not concern the fundamental nature of the aid therefore do not affect the classification of the measure.
The changes introduced by the supplementary regulation allow aid to be granted for projects that were started before the granting of the aid, indeed even before the regulation entered into force.
The amendments made by the supplementary regulation alter the fundamental features of the regulation.
In particular, the requirement of an incentive effect was removed from the regulation.
This requirement constituted an essential part of the compatibility assessment for aid measures under the originally approved scheme (see the description in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Accordingly, the Surveillance Authority takes the view that these amendments to the scheme, introduced by the supplementary regulation, constitute a fundamental change to the originally approved scheme.
In view of the fact that, under Part II, Article 1(c) of Protocol 3, changes to existing aid are to be regarded as new aid, the question arises whether the amended existing aid as a whole, or only the changes, are to be classified as new aid.
In its judgment in the Gibraltar case, the Court of First Instance ruled:
Consequently, the supervisory authority must assess whether the changes introduced by the supplementary regulation can be severed from the original scheme.
If this is the case, the amendments constitute new State aid and must therefore be reassessed in the light of Article 61(3)(c) and the Authority's Guidelines on State aid with a regional objective for 2007–2013 (the "2007 Regional Aid Guidelines").
According to settled case-law, an amendment to an existing aid scheme, by which the scheme is extended to a new category of beneficiaries, is clearly severable from the original scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries has no bearing on the assessment of the compatibility of the original scheme.
The amended wording of point (c) of Article 3 of the Investment Incentives Regulation provides that aid may also be granted for a project that was commenced before the entry into force of the supplementary regulation, but after the conclusion of a special investment agreement.
This amendment expanded the scope of the scheme and went beyond the scope set out in Decision No 390/10/COL of the Surveillance Authority, since aid could thus also be granted for projects that had already commenced prior to the scheme’s entry into force and would therefore not have passed the assessment of the incentive effect laid down in the approved scheme.
By the supplementary regulation, the scheme was thus opened to a new category of beneficiaries.
Moreover, the Surveillance Authority is of the view that the amendments to Articles 8 and 20 of the Investment Incentives Regulation introduced by the supplementary regulation, namely the reduction of the reference interest rate for corporate tax and the extension of the maximum duration of the tax exemption, were introduced solely so that this new category of beneficiaries can benefit from the scheme.
These new beneficiaries can therefore receive more aid than was the case under the originally approved scheme.
For this reason, the changes to the regulation are fundamentally interconnected.
However, these changes do not affect the compatibility of individual measures that were already introduced under the originally approved scheme.
The supervisory authority therefore considers these changes accompanying the supplementary regulation to be changes that can be clearly separated from the original regulation.
Consequently, the supervisory authority takes the view that these amendments to the scheme constitute a new aid measure.
Furthermore, it follows that any aid granted under the supplementary regulation must also be regarded as new aid.
The supervisory authority is therefore assessing the compatibility of the changes to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation.

Becromal could therefore have passed the assessment of the incentive effect required under the scheme solely on the basis of the amendments introduced by the supplementary Regulation.

For the sake of completeness and in order to address the arguments put forward by Becromal and the Icelandic authorities, the Surveillance Authority has, in the alternative, assessed the Becromal investment agreement as an ad hoc measure.
For this purpose, the supervisory authority refers to the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines and, in particular, to the requirement concerning the incentive effect contained therein.
According to point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, the existence of an incentive effect is an indispensable requirement for the compatibility of the aid:
The objective of the provisions in point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines is to enable the Surveillance Authority to verify whether an investment aid produces a sufficient incentive effect without undertaking a full analysis of the underlying economic conditions at the time of the beneficiary’s investment decision.
If such a review is not ensured before work on the project begins, then the entire project cannot receive any aid and is therefore not eligible for funding.
As confirmed by the Court in the judgment in the Kronoply case, the Surveillance Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect on a temporal sequence.
Becromal argued that the legal assessment of State aid cases had changed with the introduction of a refined economic analysis and that, when assessing the incentive effect, the Surveillance Authority must therefore also assess the economic nature of the aid (i.e., whether the aid contributed to a change in the behaviour of the aid beneficiary) and must also take into account the provisions of the LIP Guidelines.
The supervisory authority cannot accept this argument.
Becromal refers to the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: "[…] the Court of Justice, in its review of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, held that the sole decisive factor for whether the aid had an incentive effect was whether it changed the behavior of the investors, and disregarded all of the Court of First Instance’s references to the Kronoply case."
However, in its interpretation of the judgment in Case HGA, Becromal disregards the Court’s explicit view, “[…] that the requirement that the aid application must precede the commencement of the implementation of the aid is a simple, appropriate and proportionate criterion that enables the Commission to presume the necessity of the planned aid,” which was not questioned on appeal.
The Surveillance Authority therefore takes the view that, in this case, it is appropriate to base the finding of incompatibility on the chronology, namely that (i) the test of the incentive effect required by the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines was not satisfied and (ii) work on the project had already commenced before the conclusion of the investment agreement and before the aid was granted.
The Surveillance Authority also rejects Becromal’s argument that the Surveillance Authority must examine the project in light of the LIP Guidelines and apply the assessment of the incentive effect described therein.
The Surveillance Authority states that, for certain large investment projects, the assessment of the incentive effect in accordance with point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an aid application must first be submitted and eligibility established before work begins) is complemented by a second, economic assessment (based on an economic analysis of the aid’s contribution to a change in the behaviour of the aid beneficiary) in accordance with point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines and the LIP Guidelines.
The LIP Guidelines provide for an in-depth assessment for certain large investment projects, i.e., for those projects that have a significant impact on trade and could lead to substantial distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the LIP Guidelines and point 49 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment with eligible expenditure exceeding EUR 50 million.
As mentioned above, the Becromal investment agreement covers investment costs totaling EUR 117.25 million for both phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have provided the Surveillance Authority with information regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs for the project.
Furthermore, none of these parties has submitted to the Surveillance Authority information on the conditions for the obligation of detailed assessment under the LIP Guidelines, to which point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines also refer (i.e., that the aid beneficiary accounts for more than 25% of sales of the product concerned on the relevant market and that the capacity created by the investment project exceeds 5% of the market for the product concerned).
The Surveillance Authority takes the view that nothing suggests that the Becromal project exceeds the thresholds laid down in point 57 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines.
The Surveillance Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis described in the LIP Guidelines is not applicable to the Becromal investment agreement.
Consequently, according to settled case-law and the wording of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, it is sufficient to ascertain whether the aid beneficiary has submitted an aid application; moreover, the competent authority must confirm the measure in writing before the start of works; however, the aid is subject to approval by the supervisory authority.
According to the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, the "start of works" is to be understood as "the start of construction works or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding feasibility studies."
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work on the Becromal project began in 2008, and thus before the signing of the previous agreement on 7 July 2009.
The Surveillance Authority cannot accept the argument put forward by Becromal that the “start of work” should be limited to the ordering of equipment manufactured specifically for the project concerned, and that the acquisition of land and/or the construction of a factory facility does not necessarily constitute an investment decision, since such an investment could easily be resold at any time.
This interpretation is supported by the Court’s case-law, according to which even small investments by the aid beneficiary are sufficient to have legal effect; they thus constitute the first phase of the works.
Furthermore, the information available to the supervisory authority indicates that Becromal began construction of the facility in early 2008 and that the recruitment process was launched later that year.
Similarly, they pointed out that the supplementary regulation had no bearing on the Verne investment agreement, since no investment agreement had previously been concluded with Verne.
According to the Icelandic authorities, a draft investment agreement had been initialed (but not signed) on October 23, 2009.
That is why the Icelandic authorities maintained that no prior investment agreement had been concluded with Verne that could constitute a "special investment agreement" within the meaning of the supplementary regulation.
However, the Authority also noted that it did not, at the time, have sufficient information regarding the status of this project.
In their response, the Icelandic authorities stated that:
Construction work on the Becromal project began following the investment agreement concluded on 29 May 2009, that is, before the conclusion of the Becromal investment agreement on 30 December 2010,
The Kísilfélagið project had not begun at the time when the Icelandic authorities submitted their observations to the Authority.

State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement
Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement reads as follows:
The Authority will examine below whether the criteria for the existence of State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement are met for i) the scheme as amended by the supplementary regulation and ii) the five investment agreements.
To qualify as State aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of applying the rules on State aid, the term "State" also covers regional and local entities.
A loss of tax revenue amounts to the consumption of state resources in the form of tax expenditures, and public aid measures may be provided both by means of tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory or administrative nature and through the practices of the tax administration.
A reduction in the tax base, or a total or partial reduction in the amount of tax, duties, or charges, entails a loss of revenue and therefore amounts to the consumption of State resources in the form of tax expenditures.
The regime as amended by the supplementary regulation
The Authority had concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme constituted a transfer of State resources.
The changes introduced by the supplementary regulation do not alter this conclusion.
In particular, the five investment agreements contained the following exemptions in respect of taxes and charges:
The maximum corporate tax rate for the company that would invest in the project in question was set at the rate in force on the date of the signing of an agreement between the company and the Ministry of Industry;
Exemptions from the rules on depreciation were granted:
Depreciation of assets down to a zero residual value, in lieu of the 10% tax provided for by Law No. 90/2003; and
The beneficiary may, during the year in which the assets are placed in service, choose to prorate the annual depreciation rather than take a full year of depreciation, as required by Article 34 of Law No. 90/2003;
Exemptions were granted from the industrial charges set out in Law No. 134/1992 and from the market charges provided for by Law No. 160/2002;
Stamp duty on investment-related documents was set at 0.15% in lieu of the statutory stamp duty provided for by Law No. 36/1978;
the municipal property tax was set at a rate 30% lower than the maximum rate provided for in Chapter II of Law No. 4/1995;
The general social security contribution was set at a rate 20% lower than the rate provided for in Article 2(3) of Law No 113/1990;

In addition, the preamble to the investment agreement entered into with Kísilfélagið mentions an agreement between Kísilfélagið, the municipality of Reykjanesbær and the port’s investment company regarding the granting of licenses and the charges related to the project, including principles concerning property tax and land leasing.
However, the leasing of land or immovable property at a price below the market price entails a loss of revenue and therefore constitutes a transfer of State resources.
In light of the foregoing, the Authority concludes that state resources are also involved in the five investment agreements.
Measures favouring certain undertakings or the production of certain goods
The Authority concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme was selective because only undertakings investing in certain regions of Iceland eligible for aid pursuant to Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement could receive aid under the scheme.
The Authority accepts the Icelandic authorities’ argument that the amendments to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation extend to Becromal the benefits granted under the scheme.
These amendments therefore constitute a selective measure, since they concern a specific undertaking.
The Authority therefore concludes that the supplementary regulation favours certain undertakings and the production of certain goods within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Likewise, since the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are specific undertakings, the measures are selective.
The notion of aid is more general than that of subsidy, since it includes not only positive benefits, such as subsidies themselves, but also State interventions which, in various forms, alleviate the charges that normally burden an undertaking’s budget and which, thereby, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the term, are of the same nature and have identical effects.
According to settled case-law, a measure by which the public authorities grant certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although it does not involve a transfer of State resources, places the beneficiaries in a more favourable financial position than other taxpayers constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements enabled the beneficiaries to be relieved of part of the costs that they would normally have had to bear themselves in the course of their activities, such as the corporate income tax applicable at any given time, the normal municipal property tax, and the generally applicable social security contribution.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and certain productions within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the contracting parties
It is settled case-law that, for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to establish an actual effect of the aid on trade between the contracting parties and an actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether the aid is liable to affect such trade and distort competition.
Moreover, where aid granted by an EFTA State strengthens the position of an undertaking in relation to other competing undertakings in intra-EEA trade, the latter must be considered to be affected by the aid.
The Authority had concluded, in Decision No 390/10/COL, that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the beneficiary undertakings in the eligible regions compared with that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The investment agreement concluded with Becromal strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As Becromal has confirmed, the market for anode foils used in aluminium electrolytic capacitors is a global market, and among Becromal's customers are companies […] and […].
Accordingly, does the fact that Becromal’s main competitors are […] companies not mean that there is no effect on trade within the EEA?
The investment agreement entered into with Verne strengthens this company's competitive position relative to that of other data centers established in Iceland.
Furthermore, Verne operates a global wholesale data center whose services will be made available to customers within the EEA and on the global market.
The investment agreement concluded with Kísilfélagið strengthens this company's competitive position compared with that of other producers of silicon metal and silica fume.
The market for metallurgical-grade silicon and silica fume is global.
The investment agreement reached with Thorsil strengthens this company's competitive position compared with that of other silicon metal producers.
As stated in the preamble to the aforementioned investment agreement, the project was launched in particular to meet the growing demand for silicon metal in Europe and North America.
The investment agreement entered into with GMR Endurvinnslan strengthens this company’s competitive position relative to that of other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
This company is considering exporting most of its products to sell them to buyers in the construction industry.
Consequently, the Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary undertakings in the eligible regions compared with that of their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
In light of the foregoing, the Authority concludes that the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation and the five investment agreements constitute State aid within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
In its decision to open the procedure, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements fell within the scope of the scheme, since the agreements appeared, at first glance, to have been affected by the amendments to the scheme introduced by the supplementary regulation.
According to Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, "any alteration to existing aid" shall be considered new aid.
Under Article 4(1) of Decision No 195/04/COL of the Authority concerning the implementing measures referred to in Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (the 'Implementing Decision'), “[...] a modification of existing aid means any change other than changes of a purely formal or administrative nature which are not liable to affect the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market.”
Consequently, changes that do not relate to the substance of the aid do not affect the way in which the measure must be classified.
The amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation provide that aid may be granted for projects whose work began before the granting of aid — in fact, even before the scheme entered into force.
The changes introduced by the supplementary regulation alter the regime's fundamental characteristics.
In particular, the requirement for an incentive effect provided for by the scheme has been removed.
However, this requirement constituted an essential element of the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measures granted under the scheme as initially authorised (Decision No 390/10/COL).
Consequently, the Authority considers that the changes made to the scheme by the supplementary regulation constitute a substantial modification of the scheme as authorized.
Given that, under Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, any modification of existing aid must be regarded as new aid, the question arises whether the modified existing aid is to be classified as new aid, or whether this is to be the case only for the modifications as such.
In the judgment it delivered in the Gibraltar case, the Court of First Instance held as follows:
Consequently, the Authority must examine whether the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation are severable from the original scheme.
If that is the case, the amendments in themselves constitute new aid and, accordingly, must be assessed in the light of Article 61(3)(c) and the Authority's Guidelines on regional State aid for the period 2007-2013 (the "2007 Guidelines").
It is settled case-law that an amendment to an existing aid scheme providing for its extension to a new category of beneficiaries is an amendment that is clearly severable from the initial scheme, since applying the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the initial scheme.
The new wording of Article 3(c) of the Regulation on incentive measures provides that aid may also be granted to a project that began after the conclusion of a special investment agreement and before the entry into force of the supplementary regulation.
This modification broadened the scope of this scheme beyond what had been authorized by the Authority’s Decision No 390/10/COL, in that it allowed the granting of aid to projects whose works had started before the entry into force of the scheme and which, as a result, would not have met the incentive effect criterion set out in the scheme as authorized.
The supplementary regulation has therefore opened the scheme to a new category of beneficiaries.
The Authority further considers that the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation to Articles 8 and 20 of the regulation on incentive measures, namely the reduction of the corporate income tax reference rate and the extension of the maximum duration of tax exemptions, were added solely for the purpose of benefiting this new category of beneficiaries.
These new beneficiaries can therefore receive more substantial aid than was permitted under the scheme as authorized.
Thus, the changes made to the scheme are fundamentally interrelated.
They do not, however, affect the compatibility of the various measures already covered by the system as authorized.
The Authority therefore considers that the amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation constitute a modification that is clearly severable from the initial regime.

It follows, moreover, that any aid granted under the supplementary regulation will also be regarded as new aid.

To this end, the Authority refers to the 2007 Guidelines and, in particular, to the requirement of an incentive effect set out therein.
In accordance with point 30 of the 2007 guidelines, the existence of an incentive effect is a necessary precondition for compatibility:
Point 30 of the 2007 Guidelines is intended to enable the Authority to verify whether the investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without carrying out a full examination of all the economic aspects of the aid beneficiary’s investment decision at the time it was taken.
If these assurances are not provided and the work begins, “the entire project loses its entitlement to aid,” that is, the project as such is not eligible.
Thus, as confirmed by the General Court in the Kronoply judgment, the Authority may base its assessment of the incentive effect on a chronological circumstance.
Becromal argued that the assessment of State aid cases has evolved with the introduction of a more refined economic analysis, so that, when assessing the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also evaluate its economic nature (that is, verify whether it has contributed to a change in the behavior of the aid beneficiary), while also taking into account the provisions of the guidelines on large investment projects.
The Authority cannot accept this argument.
Becromal relies on the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: “[...] the Court, in its review of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, held that the only factor for determining whether the aid measure had an incentive effect was to examine whether it had altered the investor’s behaviour and set aside all the references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case.”
However, Becromal’s interpretation of the HGA judgment fails to take into account that the Court of Justice expressly held that the General Court’s conclusion that “[...] the submission of the aid application prior to the start of implementation of the investment project constitutes a simple, relevant and adequate criterion enabling the Commission to presume the necessity of the proposed aid” was not called into question on appeal.
The Authority therefore considers that, in this case, it is appropriate to base its finding of incompatibility on a chronological assessment: more specifically, i) the incentive effect criterion set out in the 2007 guidelines was not met and ii) work on the project began before the investment agreement was concluded and the aid was granted.
The Authority also rejects Becromal's argument that the Authority must examine the project in the light of the guidelines on large investment projects and apply the incentive effect criterion laid down therein.
The Authority notes that, for certain large investment projects, the incentive effect criterion set out in point 30 of the 2007 Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application be submitted and eligibility confirmed before the start of works) is complemented by a second criterion, of an economic nature (based on an economic analysis that the aid contributes to a change in the beneficiary’s behavior), set out in point 57 of the Guidelines on regional aid and in the Guidelines on large investment projects.
The guidelines on large investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain large investment projects, namely those that have substantial effects on trade and may lead to significant distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the guidelines on large investment projects and point 49 of the 2007 guidelines, a large investment project is an initial investment for which the eligible expenditure exceeds 50,000,000 EUR.
As indicated previously, the investment agreement entered into with Becromal states a total investment cost of EUR 117,250,000 for both phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have provided the Authority with any information regarding the aid intensity or the eligible costs relating to the project.
Similarly, they have provided no information on the conditions governing the obligation to carry out a detailed examination laid down in the guidelines on large investment projects, as referred to in point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 guidelines (namely, that the aid beneficiary accounts for more than 25% of sales of the products concerned on the market in question and that the capacity created by the project represents more than 5% of the market for the product concerned).
According to the Authority, there is nothing to indicate that the Becromal project exceeds the thresholds set out in point 57 of the 2007 guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth assessment provided for in the guidelines on large investment projects does not apply to the investment agreement concluded with Becromal.
Therefore, having regard to the case-law and the wording of the 2007 guidelines, it is sufficient to verify whether an aid application has been submitted by the beneficiary and whether a written confirmation from the granting authority has been given, subject to the Authority's authorization of the measure, before the start of works on the project.
According to the 2007 guidelines, the term "start of works" means "either the start of construction works or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding preliminary feasibility studies."
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work on the Becromal project had begun in 2008, even before the signing of the prior agreement dated 7 July 2009.
The Authority cannot accept Becromal’s arguments that the “start of works” should be limited to the ordering of equipment specifically designed for the project in question and that the purchase of land and/or the construction of a facility would not necessarily mark the beginning of an investment decision, because such investments could easily be sold at any time.
This interpretation is supported by the General Court’s case-law, according to which even a low level of investment by the beneficiary is sufficient to produce legal effects and is therefore considered to constitute the first stage of the works.
In addition, the information available to the Authority shows that Becromal began construction work on its facility at the beginning of 2008 and that its recruitment process began later the same year.

To qualify as State aid, an advantage must be granted by the State or through State resources.
For the purposes of applying the rules on State aid, the term 'State' also covers regional and local authorities.
A loss of tax revenue is equivalent to the consumption of State funds in the form of tax expenditures, and State aid may also be granted by means of tax provisions of a legislative, regulatory or administrative nature, as well as through practices of the tax administration.
A reduction in the tax base or a total or partial reduction in the amount of taxes, fees, or charges implies a loss of tax revenue and is therefore equivalent to the consumption of state resources in the form of tax expenditures.

To favor certain companies or productions
In Decision No 390/10/COL, the Authority concluded that the scheme was selective, in the sense that only undertakings that invest in certain regions of Iceland and that can benefit from aid pursuant to Article 61(3)(c) of the EEA Agreement could receive aid under the scheme.
The Authority accepts the Icelandic authorities' argument that the amendments to the scheme resulting from the Supplementary Regulation extended the advantages granted to Becromal under the scheme.
Therefore, those amendments constitute a selective measure, since they affect a specific company.
The Authority therefore concludes that the Supplementary Regulation favours certain undertakings and the production of certain goods within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Similarly, the beneficiaries of the five investment agreements are individual companies and, therefore, the measures are selective.
The definition of aid is broader than that of subsidy, since it embraces not only positive benefits, such as subsidies proper, but also state interventions which, in various forms, relieve the burdens that normally fall on an undertaking’s budget and which, therefore, without being subsidies in the strict sense of the word, are similar in character and produce the same effect.
According to settled case-law, a measure whereby public authorities grant certain undertakings a tax exemption which, although it does not involve a transfer of State resources, places the beneficiaries of the exemption in a more favourable financial position than other taxpayers, constitutes aid granted by the State or through State resources.
The five investment agreements allowed the beneficiaries to avoid part of the costs that they would normally have had to bear in their business activities, such as the payment of corporation tax at the rate applicable at any given time, the municipal property tax, and the generally applicable social security contribution.
The Authority therefore concludes that the five investment agreements favour certain undertakings and the production of certain goods within the meaning of Article 61(1) of the EEA Agreement.
Distortion of competition and effect on trade between the Contracting Parties
According to settled case-law, for the purposes of classifying a national measure as State aid, it is not necessary to establish the actual effect of the aid on trade between the Member Contracting Parties and the actual distortion of competition, but only to examine whether such aid is liable to affect that trade and distort competition.
Furthermore, when aid granted by an EFTA State serves to strengthen the position of an undertaking vis-à-vis other undertakings competing with it in intra-EEA trade, such trade must be considered to be affected by the aid.
The Authority concluded in Decision 390/10/COL that the scheme strengthened the competitive position of the undertakings benefiting from the aid in the eligible regions, compared with their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.
The Becromal investment agreement strengthens Becromal's competitive position relative to other producers of anodized aluminum foil for electrolytic capacitors.
As Becromal has confirmed, the market for anodized foil used in aluminum electrolytic capacitors is global, and Becromal's customers include companies in […] and […].
Thus, the fact that Becromal's main competitors are companies from […] does not mean that there are no effects on trade in the EEA.
The Verne investment agreement strengthens Verne's competitive position relative to other data centers in Iceland.
In addition, Verne operates a global wholesale data center, whose services will be available to customers in the EEA and in the global market.
The Kísilfélagið investment agreement strengthens Kísilfélagið’s competitive position relative to other producers of metallurgical silicon and silica fume.
The market for metallurgical silicon and silica powder is global.
The Thorsil investment agreement strengthens Thorsil’s competitive position relative to other silicon metal producers.
As stated in the preamble to the Thorsil investment agreement, the project was initiated, among other things, to meet increased demand for silicon metal from Europe and North America.
The GMR Endurvinnslan investment agreement strengthens GMR Endurvinnslan's competitive position relative to other steel recycling companies and steel producers.
The company intends to export most of its products to buyers in the construction industry.
Consequently, the Authority concludes that the five investment agreements strengthen the competitive position of the beneficiary companies in the eligible regions compared with their actual or potential competitors in the EEA.

In its Opening Decision, the Authority expressed doubts as to whether the aid granted under the investment agreements had been granted within the scope of the scheme, since the agreements appeared, at first sight, to have been affected by the amendments to the scheme resulting from the Supplementary Regulation.
In accordance with Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid must be considered as new aid.
In accordance with Article 4(1) of Decision No 195/04/COL of the Authority, concerning the implementing provisions of Article 27 of Part II of Protocol 3 (hereinafter the 'Implementing Decision'), ‘[…] a modification of existing aid shall mean any change other than a purely formal or administrative modification that has no bearing on the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measure with the common market’.
Therefore, modifications that do not affect the substance of the aid do not affect the way in which the measure must be classified.
The amendments introduced by the supplementary regulation allow aid to be granted to projects on which work had already begun before the granting of the aid, even before the scheme entered into force.
The modifications introduced by the supplementary regulation alter the basic characteristics of the regime.
In particular, the requirement under the incentive effect regime has been removed.
This requirement was an important part of the assessment of the compatibility of the aid measures granted under the scheme, as it was initially approved (as described in Decision No 390/10/COL).
Consequently, the Body considers that the changes to the regime introduced by the Supplementary Regulation constitute a substantial modification of the Regime as approved.
Whereas, pursuant to Article 1(c) of Part II of Protocol 3, modifications to existing aid must be regarded as new aid, the question arises whether the modified existing aid should be considered new aid, or only the modifications as such.

According to settled case-law, an amendment to an existing aid scheme that extends it to a new category of beneficiaries may be clearly severed from the initial scheme, since the application of the existing aid scheme to the new category of beneficiaries does not affect the assessment of the compatibility of the initial scheme.
The amended wording of Article 3(c) of the Regulation on incentives specifies that aid may also be granted to a project that began after the conclusion of a special investment agreement and before the entry into force of the Supplementary Regulation.

In this regard, the Body refers to the 2007 Guidelines and, in particular, to the requirement of an incentive effect set out therein.
In accordance with point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, the existence of an incentive effect is a necessary precondition for compatibility:
The objective of paragraph 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines is to enable the Authority to determine whether the investment aid has a sufficient incentive effect, without carrying out a full analysis of the economic circumstances of the beneficiary’s investment decision at the time it was taken.

Therefore, as confirmed by the General Court in the Kronoply judgment, the Body may base its assessment of the incentive effect by reference to a circumstance of a chronological nature.
Becromal has argued that the assessment of State aid cases has changed with the introduction of a more detailed economic analysis, so that when assessing the incentive effect of the aid, the Authority must also assess its economic nature (that is, whether it has contributed to a change in the behaviour of the aid beneficiary), also taking into account the provisions of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects.
The Body cannot accept this argument.
Becromal refers to the judgment of the Court of Justice in the HGA case to argue that: “[…] the Court, in its examination of the judgment of the Court of First Instance, holds that the only factor determining whether the aid measure had an incentive effect is to examine whether it changed the investor’s behaviour, and it did not take into account all the references that the Court of First Instance had made to the Kronoply case.”
However, Becromal’s interpretation of the HGA judgment fails to take into account the Court of Justice’s express statement that the General Court’s assessment that ‘[…] the lodging of the aid application prior to the start of the implementation of the investment project is a simple, relevant and appropriate criterion that enables the Commission to presume the necessity of the aid envisaged’ was not called into question in the appeal proceedings.
Therefore, the Authority takes the view that in this case it is necessary to base its finding of incompatibility on a chronological assessment, namely that (i) the incentive effect requirement set out in the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines was not met, and (ii) work on the project began before the conclusion of the investment agreement and the granting of the aid.
The Authority also rejects Becromal’s claim that the Authority must examine the project in light of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects and apply the incentive effect requirement set out therein.
The Authority notes that, for some large investment projects, the incentive effect test established in point 30 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (based on the chronological requirement that an application be submitted and eligibility be confirmed before work commences) is complemented by a second, economic test (based on an economic analysis demonstrating that the aid contributes to a change in the beneficiary’s behaviour), set out in paragraph 57 of the Guidelines on regional aid and in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects.
The Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects provide for an in-depth assessment of certain large investment projects, that is, those which have significant effects on trade and may give rise to substantial distortions of competition.
According to point 3 of the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects and point 49 of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, a large investment project means an initial investment whose eligible expenditure exceeds EUR 50 million.
As previously mentioned, the Becromal investment agreement refers to EUR 117.25 million as the total investment cost for the two phases of the project.
However, neither Becromal nor the Icelandic authorities have submitted any information to the Authority concerning the aid intensity or the eligible costs of the project.
Nor have they provided information on the conditions for the obligation of detailed verification laid down in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects referred to in point 57(a) and (b) of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines (that is, that the aid beneficiary has a share of more than 25% of the products concerned in the reference market, and that the production capacity created by the project accounts for more than 5% of the market for the reference product).
In the Authority's view, there is nothing to suggest that Becromal's project exceeds the maximum thresholds laid down in point 57 of the 2007 Guidelines.
The Authority therefore concludes that the in-depth analysis provided for in the Guidelines on regional aid for large investment projects does not apply to the Becromal investment agreement.
Therefore, in light of the case law and the wording of the 2007 Regional Aid Guidelines, it is sufficient to verify whether the application for aid has been submitted by the beneficiary and whether the aid-granting authority has issued a written confirmation, subject to the approval of the measure by the Authority, before the start of work on the project.
The 2007 guidelines define the term "start of works" as "the start of construction works, or the first firm commitment to order equipment, excluding preliminary feasibility studies."
As confirmed by the Icelandic authorities and Becromal, construction work on the Becromal project began in 2008, even before the signing of the previous agreement of 7 July 2009.
The Authority cannot accept Becromal’s argument that the “start of works” should be limited to the placing of orders for equipment specially designed for the project in question, and that the acquisition of land or the construction of a plant does not necessarily indicate the beginning of an investment decision, since such an investment could easily be sold at some point.
This interpretation is corroborated by the case-law of the General Court, according to which even a limited level of investment by the beneficiary is sufficient to produce legal effects and is therefore considered to constitute the first phase of the works.
Moreover, the information available to the Body indicates that Becromal began construction of the plant in early 2008, and its procurement procedure was initiated at the end of that year.
