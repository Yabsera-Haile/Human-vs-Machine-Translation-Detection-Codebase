In healthcare settings, microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi can spread through various routes.
Without timely and proper cleaning, healthcare workers’ hands are a major factor in the patient-to-patient transmission of microorganisms.
Timely hand hygiene can also prevent contamination of the healthcare environment (e.g., linens, surfaces, and patient-care equipment).
The Five Moments approach is designed to stop transmission at all critical moments to ensure patient safety.
During patient care, microorganisms and potential pathogens gradually colonize our hands.
Poor hand hygiene increases the risk of contamination and poses a threat to patient safety.
The purpose of practicing proper hand hygiene in this way is to remove soil, organic matter, and transient microorganisms from the hands.
It cannot eliminate the resident microbiota on the skin.
Hand hygiene is a general term that applies to handwashing, hand rubbing (e.g., using an alcohol-based handrub), or surgical hand antisepsis.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, yet even healthcare workers often overlook its importance.
Timely and effective hand hygiene protects patients, healthcare workers, and the healthcare environment from microbiological contamination by harmful pathogens.
By following recommended hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients either by preventing infections from occurring or by preventing the spread of existing ones.
Therefore, simple measures such as practicing timely and effective hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients who acquire healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).
A reduction in HAIs means fewer resources devoted to treating preventable infections, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality.
A reduction in HAIs will also reduce antibiotic use, thereby lowering antibiotic resistance.
Therefore, hand hygiene is an essential health care measure that can save lives and money!

During their work, healthcare workers repeatedly come into contact with patients and with objects and equipment in the environment.
Therefore, potential pathogens can be picked up and carried on the hands, allowing healthcare workers, when touching patients and their immediate environment, to transmit microorganisms capable of causing infection from one patient to another.
Therefore, it is very important to consider and practice hand hygiene promptly and effectively to reduce the spread of HAIs.
The Five Moments for Hand Hygiene
Healthcare workers should perform hand hygiene according to the WHO Five Moments.
The WHO Five Moments focus on contacts that occur within the patient zone during the provision of patient care.
It presents a unified vision for all healthcare workers, trainers, and observers to enhance understanding of when to clean their hands.
It consolidates all the hand hygiene indications recommended in the WHO Guidelines on Hand Hygiene and the CDC Guideline for Hand Hygiene in Health-Care Settings into the Five Moments for Hand Hygiene.
Importantly, this user- and patient-centered approach aims to enhance usability and integrate with natural workflows, making it suitable for a wide range of care settings across the healthcare industry.
The patient zone includes the patient and the surfaces and items temporarily dedicated to his or her use (shown with a dashed line in the Five Moments poster in Figure X-1).
This usually includes the patient and all inanimate surfaces they have come into contact with or had direct physical contact with, such as bed rails, bedside tables, bedding, infusion tubing, and other medical equipment.
As well as surfaces that healthcare workers frequently touch while caring for patients, such as monitors, knobs and buttons, and other touch surfaces.
Before touching a patient, please clean your hands to prevent exposing the patient to viruses carried on your hands.
Immediately clean your hands before touching any critical site that poses an infection risk to the patient, to prevent harmful bacteria—including those carried by the patient—from entering his or her body.
After a risk of exposure to body fluids
Clean your hands immediately after contact with a patient’s bodily fluids (including after removing gloves).
This action will protect you and the healthcare environment from pathogens in blood and bodily fluids.
After touching the patient and their surroundings, and when leaving the patient’s side, you must clean your hands to prevent the spread of harmful microorganisms to yourself and the healthcare environment.
Even if you have not directly touched the patient, wash your hands after touching any objects or furniture around the patient to prevent harmful microorganisms from spreading from the patient area to other parts of the healthcare environment.

Wearing gloves is an important means of protecting your hands from contamination.
If healthcare workers are wearing gloves, it does not mean they can ignore hand hygiene.
In other words, wearing gloves is no substitute for hand hygiene.
Precautions regarding hand hygiene and glove use include:
Wear gloves when there is a possibility of contact with blood or other bodily fluids, mucous membranes, nonintact skin, potentially infectious materials, or chemicals (e.g., cleaning solutions).
Perform hand hygiene before putting on gloves.
Please remove your gloves after caring for the patient.
Please do not wear the same pair of gloves when caring for multiple patients.
When wearing gloves, if moving from a contaminated body site to a clean body site (including mucous membranes, nonintact skin, or medical equipment) within the same patient or environment, remove the gloves and perform hand hygiene during patient care.
One example is to first dress a wound (non-intact skin) on the abdomen, and then perform a venotomy on the arm.
Perform hand hygiene after removing gloves.
It is important to know that, as a result of poor technique or undetected pinholes in the gloves, hand contamination frequently occurs during glove removal.
Inserting unclean hands into the box of gloves may cause microorganisms to spread throughout the entire box.
This is one of the reasons why hand hygiene should always be performed before putting on gloves and after taking them off.
To learn more about the use of gloves and other types of personal protective equipment (PPE), please consider registering for our personal protective equipment module.
Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water.
The purpose of washing your hands with soap and water is to remove dirt, organic matter, and microorganisms from your hands.
Soap helps to mechanically remove debris, loosely attached microorganisms, and substances containing fats and oils, all of which are commonly present on dirty hands.
Although the friction generated by rubbing the hands when washing with plain soap and water can remove loosely attached transient flora, the antimicrobial activity of plain soap, if present at all, is extremely low.
Proper handwashing time is important—the amount of bacteria removed by washing for 30 seconds is clearly ten times that removed in 15 seconds.
If done correctly, the entire handwashing process takes 40 to 60 seconds.
In the following situations, it is recommended to wash your hands with soap and water (rather than using an alcohol-based hand sanitizer):
If your hands are visibly soiled or contaminated with blood or other bodily fluids.
When exposure to spore-forming pathogens is suspected or confirmed, including Clostridioides difficile (a bacterial infection that can cause severe diarrhea), wash hands with soap and water because the friction generated can physically remove spores from the hands.

Use an alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) to clean your hands.
If hands are not visibly soiled, alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is the preferred method for hand hygiene because it is more effective at killing microorganisms than plain or antimicrobial soap and water, and it also has other significant advantages.
The alcohols in alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHRs) can kill microorganisms by denaturing proteins (for example, by dissolving certain microbial components).
It also has long-lasting activity, which means microorganisms take longer to proliferate on the hands.
Cleaning hands with an alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is preferred over using soap and water, including:
It takes less time—only 20 to 30 seconds.
In a busy work environment, using an alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can save you time!
Alcohol-based handrub (ABHR) can be placed directly at the point of care, making hand hygiene convenient and easy within the clinical workflow.
An alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) does not require a sink, water, or towels—hands can be air-dried.
It is important to note that the active ingredient—alcohol—is flammable, so you should be careful and ensure that alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) are stored away from sources of ignition.

Nails, nail polish and jewelry =========
Long nails, nail polish, and jewelry can compromise hand hygiene and promote bacterial growth.
The spaces and crevices beneath nail enhancements or extensions and jewelry are difficult to clean and impede access to these areas.
Natural nails should be kept relatively short, preferably not extending more than 0.5 cm (less than 1/4 inch) past the fingertips.
Wearing artificial nails or nail extensions is not recommended when working in clinical areas.
If you have nail polish on, you mustn’t rub it off.
It is strongly recommended that you do not wear any jewelry, including rings.
Additionally, you can hang the ring on a necklace and wear it around your neck.
The surgical team should remove jewelry, including rings, bracelets, and watches, before performing the surgical hand scrub.
Hand lotion and hand cream
Hand lotion and hand cream should be used with caution.
If used as shared items, lotions and tube-packaged face creams may lead to cross-contamination.
Hand lotions, hand creams, or moisturizing skin care products can be used to reduce dryness and contact dermatitis caused by frequent handwashing as part of hand hygiene.
Do not use oil-based hand creams containing petroleum jelly, as they may damage latex rubber gloves.

5.3 Exercise to evaluate a TB laboratory network
Given your enrollment in this online course, it is presumed that you are at the apex of the TB laboratory network pyramid.
For example, they may be a consultant working in the national reference laboratory or supporting national programs for the adoption of new diagnostic methods or the strengthening of the TB laboratory.
When evaluating a laboratory network from the perspective of the need to introduce new diagnostic methods, it is important to have a precise understanding of the tests being performed, at what level, for which groups, and of the integrity of the provision of diagnostic services.
As an exercise, you could ask yourself the following questions about a network of laboratories you are familiar with. This is not a complete list.
Are the WHO-recommended rapid diagnostic tests (e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP or Truenat) available in peripheral laboratories, either directly or through a specimen referral system?
Are molecular drug susceptibility tests used as the initial test to detect drug resistance in smear-positive specimens or in isolates obtained from cultures of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), instead of using culture-based phenotypic drug susceptibility tests?
Is the urine LAM-ICL test used as an aid in diagnosing active TB in HIV-positive adults, adolescents, and children with signs and symptoms of TB?
Is culture performed during treatment follow-up for patients with rifampicin-resistant TB or multidrug-resistant TB?
Are samples sent to other laboratories for sequential testing?
If so, is this being done properly (i.e., for the right tests) and efficiently?
Is a WHO-recommended rapid diagnostic test performed as the initial diagnostic test in at least 90% of notified new and relapse TB cases?
If you have answered "no" to any of the previous questions, the structure of the TB laboratory network is not optimal or it is necessary to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
