In healthcare environments, microbes such as bacteria, viruses and fungi can be spread by a variety of means.
Hands of healthcare workers are a major factor in the spread of germs between patients if they are not cleaned at the appropriate time and with the appropriate methods.
Timely hand hygiene can also prevent contamination of the medical environment (e.g. bed sheets, surfaces, patient care equipment).
The five immediate methods aim to stop transmission at all critical moments to ensure patient safety.
During the care of patients, microbes and potential pathogens gradually colonize our hands.
Lack of good hand hygiene increases the risk of contamination and poses a threat to the safety of patients.
The purpose of hand hygiene is to remove soil, organic matter and transient microorganisms from the hands.
It does not eliminate the bacteria that live on the skin.
Hand hygiene is a general term applied to the washing of hands, the rinsing of hands (e.g. with alcohol-based hand sanitizer) or the disinfection of surgical hands.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, but its importance is often overlooked even by healthcare workers.
Timely and effective hand hygiene protects patients, healthcare workers and the healthcare environment from microbial contamination from harmful pathogens.
By following recommended hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients by preventing the occurrence of infection or pre-existing transmission.
Therefore, simple measures such as timely or effective hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients who acquire a healthcare-associated infection (HAI).
Reduced HAI means fewer resources are devoted to treating preventable infections, which ultimately reduces morbidity and mortality.
Reduced HAI also reduces antibiotic use, leading to reduced antibiotic resistance.
Hand hygiene is therefore an essential health care measure that can save lives and money!

Health care workers are exposed to patients, objects and equipment in the environment.
Thus, potential pathogens can be picked up and carried around, allowing healthcare workers to transmit potentially infectious microbes from one patient to another when in contact with the patient and the surrounding environment around the patient.
Therefore, it is important to consider and effectively and timely hand hygiene to reduce the spread of HAI.
5 moments of hand hygiene
Healthcare workers should use hand hygiene according to WHO guidelines on 5 occasions.
WHO 5 Moments focuses on the contact that occurs within the *patient area* during the provision of patient care.
It offers a unified vision for all healthcare workers, trainers and observers to improve understanding of when to wash hands.
It combines all the hand hygiene instructions recommended in the WHO Hands on Hands Guidelines and the CDC Hands on Hands Guidelines for Health Care Facilities into the Five Moments of Need for Hand Hygiene.
Importantly, this user- and patient-centric approach aims to simplify usability and add it to natural workflows, thus making it applicable to a wide range of care environments and the healthcare industry.

The use of gloves is an important tool to protect hands from contamination.
The fact that healthcare workers wear gloves does not mean that they can ignore hand hygiene.
In other words, wearing gloves is not a substitute for hand hygiene.
Hand hygiene and glove use precautions include:
* Wear gloves if you may come into contact with blood or other body fluids, mucous membranes, incomplete skin, potentially infectious substances or chemicals (e.g. cleaning fluids).
Hand hygiene is recommended before gloves are worn.
Remove gloves after you have treated the patient.
Do not use the same pair of gloves for multiple patients.
When wearing gloves, if the gloves are moved from a contaminated body part to another non-contaminated body part (including mucous membranes, incomplete skin or medical devices in the same patient or environment), they should be removed and hand hygiene should be performed during the care of the patient.
An example is a wound dressing on the abdomen (not the whole skin) followed by a vein cut on the arm.
Hand hygiene should be performed after removing the gloves.
It is important to know that hand contamination is often caused by poor technology or by the absence of small holes in the gloves.
If you reach for the box with unclean hands, it can spread germs throughout the box.
This is one of the reasons why it is important to always clean your hands before and after wearing gloves.

Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water
Hand washing with soap and water is intended to remove dirt, organic matter and microorganisms from the hands.
Soap helps to mechanically remove debris, loose soil-bound microbes, and substances containing fats and oils, which are often found on dirty hands.
Although the friction generated by rubbing hands with ordinary soap and water can remove loosely attached fleas, the antibacterial activity of ordinary soap is very low even in the presence of the bacteria.
The correct washing time is important, as 30 seconds of hand washing removes 10 times the amount of bacteria as 15 seconds of hand washing.
If correct, the entire hand washing process takes 40 to 60 seconds.
Hand washing with soap and water is recommended (not alcohol-based hand sanitizer) in the following situations:
* If your hands are marked with blood or other bodily fluids.
When suspected or confirmed exposure to pathogens that cause ringworm, including the indistinguishable fungus, which can cause severe diarrhea, as the frictional force of washing hands with soap and water can physically remove the ringworm from the hand.

Alcohol-based hand sanitizer for cleaning hands (ABHR)
If the hands are not visibly dirty, alcohol-based hand sanitizers are the first choice for hand cleaning, as alcohol-based hand sanitizers (ABHR) are more effective at killing microbes than regular or antibacterial soap and water, and they have other significant advantages.
The alcohols in alcohol-based hand sanitizer (ABHR) kill microbes by altering the proteins (e.g., they dissolve certain microbial components).
It also has a persistent activity, which means that the microbes need more time to reproduce on the hand.
Alcohol-based hand sanitizer (ABHR) is a better way to clean hands than soap and water, including:
* It takes less time to cook, only 20 to 30 seconds.
In a busy work environment, using alcohol-based hand sanitizer (ABHR) can save you time!
Alcoholic hand sanitizer (ABHR) can be placed directly at the point of care, facilitating easy access to hand hygiene in the clinical workflow.
Alcohol-based hand washing solution (ABHR) does not require a sink, and can be air dried with water or towel wash.
It is important to note that the active ingredient in alcohol hydrochloride is flammable, so you need to be careful and make sure that the alcohol hydrochloride solution (ABHR) is stored away from the source of the fire.

Exercise to evaluate a network of TB laboratories
By enrolling in this online course, you are assumed to be part of the peak of the TB laboratory network pyramid.
For example, you may be a consultant working in the national reference laboratory or supporting national programmes on the adoption of new diagnostic methods or the strengthening of the TB laboratory.
When assessing a network of laboratories from the perspective of the need to introduce new diagnostic methods, it is important to have a precise understanding of the tests being carried out, at what level, for which groups and the integrity of the provision of diagnostic services.
As an exercise, you could ask yourself the following questions about a network of laboratories that you know well.
* Are the WHO recommended PDRs (e.g. Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP or Truenat) available from the peripheral laboratories either directly or through a sample referral system?
Are molecular drug sensitivity tests used as the initial test for detecting drug resistance in bacillus positive samples or in culture isolates of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), instead of using phenotypic culture-based drug sensitivity tests?
Is the urine LAM-ICL test used as an aid to the diagnosis of active TB in the HIV-positive adult, adolescent and child population with signs and symptoms of TB?
Is culture performed during follow-up of patients with rifampicin-resistant or multidrug-resistant TB?
Are samples routed to other laboratories for subsequent testing?
If so, is this done in an appropriate manner (i.e. for the correct tests) and efficiently?
Is the WHO recommended PDR as an initial diagnostic test performed in at least 90% of new and relapsed TB cases reported?
If you answered 'no' to any of the above questions, the structure of the TB laboratory network is not optimal or there is a need to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
