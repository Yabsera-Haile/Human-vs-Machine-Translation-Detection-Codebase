In healthcare settings, microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi can spread through various pathways.
Healthcare workers' hands are a major factor in the transmission of microorganisms between patients if proper cleaning methods are not used at appropriate times.
Timely hand hygiene can also prevent contamination of the healthcare environment (such as bed linens, surfaces, and patient care equipment).
The 5 Moments approach aims to stop transmission at all critical moments to ensure patient safety.
During patient care, microorganisms and potential pathogens gradually colonize on our hands.
Lack of good hand hygiene increases the risk of contamination and poses a threat to patient safety.
The purpose of performing proper hand hygiene is to remove soil, organic matter, and transient microorganisms from the hands.
It cannot eliminate the resident bacterial flora living on the skin.
Hand hygiene is a general term that applies to handwashing, hand rubbing (for example, using alcohol-based hand rub), or surgical hand disinfection.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, yet even healthcare workers often overlook its importance.
Timely and effective hand hygiene protects patients, healthcare workers, and the healthcare environment from microbial contamination by harmful pathogens.
By following recommended hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients by preventing infections from occurring or preventing the spread of existing ones.
Therefore, simple methods like timely and effective hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients acquiring healthcare-associated infections (HAI).
The reduction of HAI means fewer resources are used to treat preventable infections, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality.
Reducing HAIs will also reduce antibiotic use, thereby leading to decreased antibiotic resistance.
Therefore, hand hygiene is an essential healthcare measure that can save lives and money!

The 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene
Healthcare workers should perform hand hygiene according to the "WHO 5 Moments".
The WHO 5 Moments focus on contact that occurs *within the patient zone* during the provision of patient care.
It presents a unified vision for all healthcare workers, trainers, and observers to enhance understanding of when to clean hands.
It consolidates all hand hygiene indications recommended in WHO's Guidelines on Hand Hygiene and CDC's Guideline for Hand Hygiene in Healthcare Settings into the five moments when hand hygiene is required.
Importantly, this user- and patient-centered approach is designed to simplify ease of use and integrate into natural workflows, making it applicable to a wide range of care settings and the healthcare industry.
The patient zone includes the patient and the surfaces and items temporarily dedicated to him/her (shown with dotted lines in the 5 moments poster in Figure X-1).
This typically includes the patient and all inanimate surfaces that they have touched or had direct physical contact with, such as bedside rails, bedside tables, bedding, infusion tubes, and other medical equipment.
As well as surfaces frequently touched by healthcare workers when caring for patients, such as monitors, knobs and buttons, and other touch surfaces.
Please clean your hands before touching patients to prevent them from being infected by viruses carried on your hands.
Clean your hands immediately before touching critical sites with infectious risk to the patient to prevent harmful germs, including the patient's own, from infecting him/her.
After the risk of bodily fluid contact**
Hands should be cleaned immediately after contact with patient body fluids (including after removing gloves).
This action will protect you and the medical environment from pathogens in blood and body fluids.
Clean your hands after touching the patient and their surroundings, and when leaving the patient's side, to prevent yourself and the healthcare environment from being contaminated by harmful microorganisms.
Even without direct contact with patients, hands should be washed after touching any objects or furniture around the patient to prevent harmful microorganisms from spreading from the patient area to other parts of the healthcare environment.

Wearing gloves is an important tool for protecting hands from contamination.
If healthcare workers wear gloves, it does not mean they can ignore hand hygiene.
In other words, wearing gloves is not a substitute for hand hygiene.
Hand hygiene and glove use precautions include:
When there is potential contact with blood or other body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, potentially infectious materials, or chemicals (such as cleaning solutions), please wear gloves.
Perform hand hygiene before putting on gloves.
Please remove your gloves after caring for patients.
Do not wear the same pair of gloves to care for multiple patients.
When wearing gloves, if moving from a contaminated body site to another non-contaminated body site (including mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or medical devices within the same patient or environment), gloves should be removed and hand hygiene performed during patient care.
One example is first applying a wound dressing on the abdomen (non-intact skin), then performing a venipuncture on the arm.
Hand hygiene should be performed after removing gloves.
It is important to know that hand contamination often occurs during glove removal due to poor technique or undetected small holes in the gloves.
Unclean hands reaching into the glove box may spread microorganisms throughout the entire box.
This is one of the reasons why hand hygiene must always be performed before and after wearing gloves.
To learn more about glove use and other types of personal protective equipment (PPE), please consider registering for our personal protective equipment module.
Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water.
The purpose of washing hands with soap and water is to remove dirt, organic matter, and microorganisms from the hands.
Soap helps mechanically remove debris, loosely attached microorganisms, and substances containing fats and oils that are commonly found on dirty hands.
Although the friction generated by rubbing hands when washing with plain soap and water can remove loosely attached transient flora, the antimicrobial activity of plain soap is minimal, if present at all.
Proper handwashing duration is important—washing hands for 30 seconds removes 10 times more bacteria than washing for 15 seconds.
If done correctly, the entire handwashing process should take 40 to 60 seconds.
In the following situations, it is recommended to wash hands with soap and water (rather than using alcohol-based hand sanitizer):
If hands are visibly soiled, or contaminated with blood or other body fluids.
When exposure to spore-forming pathogens is suspected or confirmed, *including Clostridium difficile* (a bacterial infection that can cause severe diarrhea), handwashing with soap and water is recommended because the friction generated during handwashing can physically remove spores from the hands.

Clean hands with alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR)
If hands are not visibly soiled, alcohol-based hand sanitizer is the preferred method for hand hygiene, as alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is more effective at killing microorganisms than regular or antimicrobial soap and water, and it also has other significant advantages.
Alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) kill microorganisms by denaturing proteins through the alcohol they contain (for example, they dissolve certain microbial components).
It also has long-lasting activity, which means microorganisms take longer to multiply on hands.
Using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) to clean hands is superior to using soap and water in the following ways:
* Less time required—only 20 to 30 seconds.
In busy work environments, using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can save you time!
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can be placed directly at the point of care, making hand hygiene convenient and easy to perform within clinical workflows.
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) does not require a sink, water, or towels—hands can be air-dried.
It should be noted that the active ingredient—alcohol—is flammable, so you need to be careful and ensure that alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is stored away from sources of ignition.

Nails, Nail Polish and Jewelry =========
Long fingernails, nail polish, and jewelry can affect hand hygiene and promote bacterial growth.
The spaces and gaps under nail polish, nail extensions, and jewelry are difficult to clean and hinder access to these areas.
Natural nails should be kept short, preferably not extending more than 0.5 cm (less than 1/4 inch) beyond the fingertip.
When working in clinical areas, artificial nails or nail extensions are not recommended.
If you've applied nail polish, you must not let it get worn off.
It is strongly recommended not to wear any jewelry, including rings.
Additionally, you can also hang the ring on a necklace and wear it around your neck.
The surgical team should remove jewelry, including rings, bracelets, and watches, before performing surgical hand cleaning.
Hand lotion and hand cream
Hand lotions and hand creams should be used with caution.
If used as shared products, lotions and tube creams may cause cross-contamination.
Hand lotions, hand creams, or moisturizing skin care products can be used to reduce dryness and contact dermatitis caused by hand hygiene due to frequent handwashing.
Do not use oil-based hand creams containing petroleum jelly, as they may damage latex rubber gloves.

5.3 Exercise to evaluate a TB laboratory network
Given your enrollment in this online course, it is assumed that you are part of the top of the TB laboratory network pyramid.
For example, you may be a consultant working in the national reference laboratory or providing support to national programs on the adoption of new diagnostic methods or strengthening of the TB laboratory.
When evaluating a laboratory network from the perspective of the need to introduce new diagnostic methods, it is important to have a precise understanding of the tests being performed, at what level, for which groups, and of the integrity of diagnostic service delivery.
As an exercise, you could ask yourself the following questions about a network of laboratories that you know well. This is not a complete list.
Are WHO-recommended rapid diagnostic tests (e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP, or Truenat) available in peripheral laboratories, either directly or through a specimen referral system?
Are molecular drug susceptibility tests used as the initial test to detect drug resistance in smear-positive specimens or in isolates obtained from cultures of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), instead of using culture-based phenotypic drug susceptibility tests?
Is the LAM-ICL urine test used as an aid in diagnosing active TB in the adult, adolescent, and pediatric HIV-positive population with signs and symptoms of TB?
Is culture performed during treatment monitoring of patients with rifampicin-resistant TB or multidrug-resistant TB?
Are samples sent to other laboratories to perform subsequent tests?
If so, is this done appropriately (that is, for the correct tests) and efficiently?
Is a WHO-recommended PDR performed as an initial diagnostic test in at least 90% of notified new and relapsed TB cases?
If you have answered "no" to any of the previous questions, the structure of the TB laboratory network is not optimal or it is necessary to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
