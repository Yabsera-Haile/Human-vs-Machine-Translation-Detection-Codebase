In the medical care environment, microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi can spread through various pathways.
If healthcare workers do not use appropriate methods for cleaning at the appropriate time, their hands can be a major factor in the spread of microorganisms among patients.
Proper hand hygiene can prevent contamination of the medical environment (for example, bed linens, surfaces, and patient care equipment).
5 instant methods aim to stop the spread at all key moments to ensure patient safety.
During patient care, microorganisms and potential pathogens gradually colonize our hands.

Hand hygiene is a general term that applies to handwashing, hand rubbing (for example, using an alcohol-based hand rub) or surgical hand antisepsis.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, but even healthcare workers often overlook its importance.
Effective and timely hand hygiene protects patients, healthcare workers, and the healthcare environment from microbial contamination by harmful pathogens.
By following recommended hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients by preventing the occurrence of infections or preventing the spread of existing ones.
Therefore, simple methods like timely or effective hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients acquiring healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).
HAIs reduction means fewer resources are used to treat preventable infections, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality.
HAI reduction will further reduce the use of antibiotics, thereby reducing antibiotic resistance.
Therefore, hand hygiene is an indispensable health care measure, it can save lives and money!

5 Moments for Hand Hygiene
Healthcare workers should perform hand hygiene according to the WHO 5 Moments.
WHO 5 key moments focus on contact that occurs in the *patient area* during the provision of patient care.
It put forward a unified vision for all medical staff, trainees, and observers to enhance understanding of when to clean hands.
It integrates all hand hygiene guidance information recommended in the WHOs Hand Hygiene Guidelines and the CDCs Hand Hygiene in Healthcare Settings into the five key moments for hand hygiene.
It is important that this user- and patient-centered approach aims to simplify usability and integrate it into natural workflows, making it applicable to a wide range of care environments and healthcare industries.
The patient area includes the patient and any surfaces and objects temporarily belonging to him/her (shown by dashed lines in the 5-moment poster in Figure X-1).
This usually includes all inanimate surfaces that the patient has come into contact with or had direct physical contact with, such as bed rails, bedside tables, bedding, intravenous tubes, and other medical equipment.
As well as surfaces frequently touched by medical staff when caring for patients, such as monitors, knobs, buttons, and other touch surfaces.
Before contacting patients, please clean your hands first to prevent patients from being harmed by viruses carried on your hands.
Before contacting key body parts of a patient that pose an infection risk, immediately clean your hands to prevent the patient from being harmed by harmful bacteria, including harmful bacteria carried by the patient themselves that could harm their own body.
After bodily fluid contact risk
After contact with patient body fluids (including after removing gloves), clean hands immediately.
This action will protect you and the medical environment from the invasion of pathogens in blood and body fluids.
After touching the patient and their surroundings, and when leaving the patients side, hands must be cleaned to prevent oneself and the medical environment from being harmed by harmful microorganisms.
Even without direct contact with patients, wash your hands after contact with any objects or furniture surrounding the patient to prevent harmful microorganisms from spreading from the patient area to other parts of the medical environment.

Using gloves is an important tool for protecting hands from contamination.
If healthcare workers wear gloves, it does not mean that hand hygiene can be ignored.
In other words, wearing gloves does not replace hand hygiene.
Hand hygiene and glove use precautions include:
When handling blood or other bodily fluids, mucous membranes, incomplete skin, potentially infectious materials, or chemicals (e.g., cleaning solutions), wear gloves.
Wash your hands before putting on gloves.
Remove gloves after caring for the patient.
Do not wear the same pair of gloves to care for multiple patients.
When wearing gloves, if moving from a contaminated body site to another uncontaminated body site (including the same patient or mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or medical devices within the environment), hand hygiene should be performed after removing the gloves during patient care.
One example is to first perform a wound dressing on the abdomen (non-intact skin), and then perform a vein incision on the arm.
After removing gloves, perform hand hygiene.
It is important to know that hand contamination often occurs during glove removal due to poor technique or undetected small holes in the gloves.
Putting an unclean hand into the glove box may spread microorganisms throughout the box.
This is one of the reasons why hand hygiene cleaning should always be performed before and after wearing gloves.
For more information about glove use and other types of personal protective equipment (PPE), consider registering for our personal protective equipment module.
Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water.
Washing hands with soap and water is to remove dirt, organic matter, and microorganisms from the hands.
Soap helps mechanically remove debris, loosely attached microorganisms, and substances containing fats and oils, which are commonly found on dirty hands.
Although friction generated by rubbing hands with ordinary soap and water can remove loosely attached transient microbial populations, the antibacterial activity of ordinary soap, even if it exists, is extremely low.
Correct handwashing time is important - washing hands for 30 seconds removes 10 times the amount of bacteria compared to washing hands for 15 seconds.
If it is correct, the whole handwashing process needs 40 to 60 seconds.
In the following cases, it is recommended to wash hands with soap and water (instead of using alcohol-based hand sanitizer):
If both hands have obvious stains, or are contaminated with blood or other bodily fluids.
When suspicion or confirmation of exposure to spore-forming pathogens, *including Clostridioides difficile* (a bacterium that can cause severe diarrhea), occurs, friction generated during handwashing with soap and water can physically remove spores from the hands.

Clean hands with alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR)
If hands are not visibly soiled, alcohol-based hand sanitizer is the preferred method for cleaning hands because alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is more effective than soap and water, ordinary or antibacterial, at killing microorganisms, and it has other significant advantages.
Alcohol in alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) can kill microorganisms, such as by denaturing proteins (for example, by dissolving certain components of microorganisms).
It also has lasting activity, which means microorganisms take longer to reproduce on the hands.
Using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) to clean hands is superior to using soap and water, including:
Less time required - only 20 to 30 seconds.
In a busy work environment, using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can save you time!
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can be directly placed at the care point, making hand hygiene convenient and easy to perform in the clinical workflow.
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) does not require a sink, water, or towels - hands can air dry.
It is important to note that the active ingredient—alcohol—is flammable, so you need to be careful and ensure that alcohol-based hand sanitizer (ABHR) is stored away from fire sources.

Fingernails, nail polish, and jewelry
Long fingernails, nail polish, and jewelry can affect hand hygiene and promote bacterial growth.
The gaps under nail polish or extended nail tips and jewelry are difficult to clean and hinder access to these areas.
Natural fingernails should be kept short, preferably not exceeding 0.5 centimeters (less than 1/4 inch) beyond the fingertip.
It is not recommended to use artificial nails or nail extensions when working in a clinical area.
If you have painted your nails with nail polish, you should not file them.
It is strongly recommended not to wear jewelry, including rings.
Alternatively, you can also wear the ring on a necklace around your neck.
The surgical team should remove jewelry, including rings, bracelets, and watches, before performing hand hygiene.
Hand cream and hand lotion
Hand cream and hand lotion should be used with caution.
Lotions and tube-shaped creams may cause cross-contamination if used as shared products.
Hand creams, hand lotions, or moisturizing skin care products can be used to reduce dryness and contact dermatitis caused by hand hygiene due to frequent handwashing.
Do not use oily hand creams containing petroleum jelly, as they may damage latex rubber gloves.

5.3 Exercise to evaluate a network of TB laboratories
Given your enrollment in this online course, it is presumed that you are part of the top of the pyramid of the TB laboratory network.
For example, it can be a consultant who works in the national reference laboratory or who provides support to national programs on the adoption of new diagnostic methods or the strengthening of the TB laboratory.
When evaluating a network of laboratories from the perspective of the need to introduce new diagnostic methods, it is important to have a precise understanding of the tests performed, at what level, for what groups, and of the integrity of the provision of diagnostic services.
As an exercise, you could ask yourself the following questions about a network of laboratories that you know well. This is not an exhaustive list.
Are the WHO-recommended DR-TB tests (e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP or Truenat) available in peripheral laboratories, either directly or through a sample referral system?
Are molecular drug sensitivity tests used as an initial test to detect drug resistance in samples with a positive baciloscopy or in isolates obtained by culture of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), instead of using phenotypic drug sensitivity tests based on culture?
Is the LAM-ICL urine test used as an aid in the diagnosis of active TB in the adult, adolescent, and pediatric HIV-positive population with signs and symptoms of TB?
Is the culture performed during the follow-up of patients with rifampicin-resistant TB or multidrug-resistant TB?
Are samples sent to other laboratories for subsequent testing?
If so, is this done appropriately (that is, for the correct tests) and efficiently?
Is a WHO-recommended PPD performed as an initial diagnostic test in at least 90% of new and relapse TB cases reported?
If you answered no to any of the previous questions, the laboratory network structure for TB is not optimal or it is necessary to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
