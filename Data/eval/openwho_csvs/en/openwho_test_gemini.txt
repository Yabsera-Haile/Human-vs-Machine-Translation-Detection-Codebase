In a healthcare environment, microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi can spread through various routes.
If not cleaned with the proper methods at the appropriate times, the hands of healthcare workers are a major factor in the transmission of microorganisms between patients.
Timely hand hygiene also prevents contamination of the medical environment (e.g., bed linens, surfaces, patient care equipment).
The 5 Moments method is designed to stop transmission at all key moments to ensure patient safety.
During patient care, microorganisms and potential pathogens gradually colonize our hands.
Poor hand hygiene increases the risk of contamination and poses a threat to patient safety.

It cannot eliminate the resident flora living on the skin.
Hand hygiene is a general term that applies to handwashing, handrubbing (for example, using an alcohol-based handrub), or surgical hand antisepsis.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, but even healthcare workers often neglect its importance.
Timely and effective hand hygiene protects patients, healthcare workers, and the healthcare environment from microbial contamination with harmful pathogens.
By following recommended hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients by preventing the occurrence or spread of infections.
Therefore, simple methods such as timely and effective hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients who acquire healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).
A reduction in HAIs means fewer resources are used to treat preventable infections, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality.
Reducing HAIs also reduces antibiotic use, thereby reducing antibiotic resistance.
Therefore, hand hygiene is an essential healthcare measure that saves lives and money

In the course of their work, healthcare workers repeatedly come into contact with patients, as well as objects and equipment in the environment.
Therefore, potential pathogens can be picked up and carried, allowing healthcare workers to transmit infectious microorganisms from one patient to another through contact with patients and their surroundings.
Therefore, it is very important to perform hand hygiene in a timely and effective manner to reduce the transmission of HAIs.
The 5 moments of hand hygiene
Healthcare workers should perform hand hygiene according to the WHO's 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene.
The WHO 5 Moments focus on contact that occurs *within the patient zone* during patient care.
It presents a unified vision for all healthcare workers, trainers, and observers to enhance understanding of when to clean their hands.
It combines all the hand hygiene indications recommended in the WHO's Guidelines on Hand Hygiene and the CDC's Guideline for Hand Hygiene in Health-Care Settings into the 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene.
Importantly, this user- and patient-centric approach is designed to enhance ease of use and integrate into natural workflows, making it suitable for a wide range of care settings and the healthcare industry.
The patient zone includes the patient and the surfaces and items temporarily dedicated to him/her (shown with a dotted line in the 5 Moments poster in Figure X-1).
This usually includes the patient and all inanimate surfaces they have touched or had direct physical contact with, such as bed rails, bedside tables, bedding, IV tubing, and other medical equipment.
As well as surfaces frequently touched by healthcare workers when caring for patients, such as monitors, knobs, buttons, and other touch surfaces.
Before touching a patient, please clean your hands to prevent them from being harmed by viruses carried on your hands.
Clean your hands immediately before touching a critical site with an infection risk to the patient, to protect the patient from harmful germs, including those from the patient's own body.
After a risk of exposure to body fluids.
Clean your hands immediately after contact with a patient's bodily fluids (including after removing gloves).
This action will protect you and the medical environment from pathogens in blood and bodily fluids.
Clean your hands after touching a patient and their surroundings, and when leaving their side, to protect yourself and the healthcare environment from harmful microorganisms.
Even without direct contact with the patient, wash your hands after touching any object or furniture in the patient's surroundings to prevent harmful microorganisms from spreading from the patient area to other parts of the healthcare environment.

Using gloves is an important tool for protecting hands from contamination.
If healthcare workers are wearing gloves, it does not mean they can neglect hand hygiene.
In other words, wearing gloves is not a substitute for hand hygiene.
Precautions for hand hygiene and glove use include:
Wear gloves when there is potential contact with blood or other body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, potentially infectious materials, or chemicals (for example, cleaning solutions).
Perform hand hygiene before putting on gloves.
Please remove your gloves after caring for the patient.
Do not wear the same pair of gloves to care for multiple patients.
During patient care, gloves should be removed and hand hygiene performed when moving from a contaminated body site to a clean body site on the same patient or in the environment (including mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or medical devices).
An example is applying a wound dressing to the abdomen (non-intact skin), and then performing venipuncture on the arm.
Perform hand hygiene after removing gloves.
It is important to know that hand contamination often occurs during glove removal as a result of poor technique or undetected small holes in the gloves.
Unclean hands reaching into the glove box may spread microorganisms throughout the entire box.
This is one of the reasons why hand hygiene should always be performed before putting on and after removing gloves.
To learn more about glove use and other types of personal protective equipment (PPE), consider registering for our Personal Protective Equipment module.
Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water.
The purpose of washing hands with soap and water is to remove dirt, organic matter, and microorganisms from the hands.
Soap helps to mechanically remove debris, loosely attached microorganisms, and substances containing fats and oils, which are commonly found on dirty hands.
Although the friction from rubbing hands while washing with plain soap and water can remove loosely attached transient flora, the antimicrobial activity of plain soap is minimal, if any.
The correct duration for handwashing is important—washing for 30 seconds removes ten times more bacteria than washing for 15 seconds.
If done correctly, the entire hand-washing process takes 40 to 60 seconds.
In the following situations, it is recommended to wash your hands with soap and water (rather than using an alcohol-based hand rub):
If hands are visibly soiled, or contaminated with blood or other body fluids.
When exposure to spore-forming pathogens is suspected or confirmed, *including Clostridioides difficile* (a bacterial infection that can cause severe diarrhea), the friction from washing hands with soap and water can physically remove the spores from the hands.

Clean hands with an alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR).
If hands are not visibly soiled, an alcohol-based hand sanitizer is the preferred choice for cleaning them, because alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) are more effective at killing microorganisms than plain or antimicrobial soap and water, and they also have other significant advantages.
The alcohol in alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) kills microorganisms by denaturing proteins (for example, it dissolves certain microbial components).
It also has long-lasting activity, which means it takes longer for microorganisms to multiply on the hands.
Using an alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) to clean hands is superior to using soap and water, including:
It takes less time—only 20 to 30 seconds.
In a busy work environment, using an alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can save you time
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can be placed directly at the point of care, making it convenient and easy to perform hand hygiene within the clinical workflow.
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) does not require a sink, water, or towels—hands can air dry.
It should be noted that the active ingredient—alcohol—is flammable, so you need to be careful and ensure that alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) is stored away from sources of fire.

Nails, Nail Polish, and Jewelry
Long fingernails, nail polish, and jewelry can affect hand hygiene and promote bacterial growth.
The spaces and crevices under nail art, nail extensions, and jewelry are difficult to clean and hinder access to these areas.
Natural nails should be kept short, preferably not extending more than 0.5 cm (less than 1/4 inch) beyond the fingertip.
The use of artificial nails or nail extensions is not recommended when working in clinical areas.
If you've painted your nails, don't scuff them.
It is strongly advised not to wear any jewelry, including rings.
Alternatively, you can hang the ring on a necklace and wear it around your neck.
The surgical team should remove jewelry, including rings, bracelets, and watches, before performing the surgical hand scrub.
Hand lotion and hand cream
Hand lotion and hand cream should be used with caution.
If used as shared products, lotions and tube-packaged face creams may cause cross-contamination.
Hand lotion, hand cream, or moisturizing skin care products can be used to reduce dryness and contact dermatitis caused by frequent hand washing.
Do not use oil-based hand creams containing petroleum jelly, as they may damage latex gloves.

5.3 Exercise for evaluating a network of TB laboratories
Given your enrollment in this online course, it is assumed that you are part of the top of the TB laboratory network pyramid.
For example, they may be a consultant who works at the national reference laboratory or supports national programs on the adoption of new diagnostic methods or the strengthening of the TB laboratory.
When evaluating a laboratory network from the perspective of the need to introduce new diagnostic methods, it is important to have a precise understanding of the tests that are performed, at what level, for which groups, and the integrity of the diagnostic service delivery.
As an exercise, you could ask yourself the following questions about a laboratory network that you know well. This is not a complete list.
Are the WHO-recommended RDTs (e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP, or Truenat) available in peripheral laboratories, either directly or through a sample referral system?
Are molecular drug susceptibility tests used as an initial test to detect drug resistance in smear-positive samples or in isolates from Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) cultures, instead of using culture-based phenotypic drug susceptibility tests?
Is the urine LAM-ICL test used as an aid in the diagnosis of active TB in HIV-positive adults, adolescents, and children with signs and symptoms of TB?
Is culture performed during treatment follow-up for patients with rifampicin-resistant TB or multidrug-resistant TB?
Are the samples sent to other laboratories for consecutive tests?
If so, is this done properly (i.e., for the right tests) and efficiently?
Is a WHO-recommended rapid diagnostic test performed as an initial diagnostic test in at least 90% of notified new and relapse TB cases?
If you have answered "no" to any of the previous questions, the structure of the TB laboratory network is not optimal or it is necessary to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
