Microbes such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi can spread through various pathways in healthcare environments.
Failure to use appropriate methods at the right time for hand hygiene makes healthcare workers hands a major factor in the transmission of microbes between patients.
Timely hand hygiene can also prevent contamination of the medical environment (such as bed sheets, surfaces, patient care equipment).
Five moment methods aim to stop transmission at all critical moments to ensure patient safety.
Microorganisms and potential pathogens gradually colonize our hands during patient care.
Poor hand hygiene increases the risk of contamination and poses a threat to patient safety.
The purpose of good hand hygiene is to remove soil, organic matter, and transient microorganisms from the hands.
It cannot eliminate the resident skin microbiota.
Hand hygiene is a generic term applicable to hand washing, hand rubbing (such as with an alcohol-based hand rub) or surgical hand antisepsis.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, yet even healthcare workers often overlook its importance.
Effective hand hygiene protects patients, healthcare workers, and the healthcare environment from microbial contamination by harmful pathogens.
By following recommended hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients by preventing the occurrence of infections or the spread of existing ones.
Therefore, simple methods like hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients acquiring healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).
The decrease in HAI means fewer resources used for treating preventable infections, ultimately leading to a reduction in incidence and mortality.
Reducing HAI would also reduce the use of antibiotics, thereby decreasing antibiotic resistance.
Therefore, hand hygiene is an essential health care measure that can save lives and money!

Five Moments for Hand Hygiene
Nurses and medical workers should perform hand hygiene according to the WHO 5 moments.
WHO emphasizes five moments of contact that occur within the patient area during the provision of patient care.
It presents a unified vision for all healthcare workers, trainers, and observers to enhance understanding of when to clean their hands.
It combines all hand hygiene guidance recommended in WHOs Guidelines on Hand Hygiene in Health-care Settings and CDCs Guideline for Hand Hygiene in Healthcare Settings at five moments requiring hand hygiene.
What is important is that this user and patient-centered approach aims to simplify usability and integrate it into natural workflow processes, making it applicable to a wide range of care environments and the healthcare industry.
*The patient area includes the patient and surfaces and items temporarily belonging to him/her (shown with dashed lines in poster X-1 at five moments).
This typically includes patients and all inanimate surfaces that they come into contact with or physically touch, such as bedside rails, bedside tables, bed linens, IV tubes, and other medical equipment.
As well as surfaces that healthcare workers frequently come into contact with when caring for patients, such as monitors, knobs and buttons, and other touch surfaces.
Wash your hands before contacting the patient to prevent the patients from being infected by viruses carried on your hands.
Wash hands immediately before contacting key patient areas with infection risk to prevent harmful bacteria, including harmful bacteria carried by the patient themselves, from harming the patient.
Risk of Bodily Fluid Exposure
Clean hands immediately after contacting patient fluids (including after removing gloves).
This gesture protects you and the healthcare environment from pathogens in blood and body fluids.
Clean hands after touching patients and their surroundings, as well as upon leaving their vicinity, to prevent harmful microorganisms from affecting oneself and the medical environment.
Even after touching any object or furniture near a patient, one must wash hands to prevent harmful microorganisms from spreading from the patient area to other parts of the healthcare environment.

Using gloves is an important tool for protecting hands from contamination.
Wearing gloves by healthcare workers does not mean neglecting hand hygiene.
In other words, wearing gloves does not replace hand hygiene.
Hand hygiene and glove use precautions include:
Wear gloves when contact is possible with blood or other body fluids, mucous membranes, incomplete skin, potentially infectious substances, or chemicals (such as cleaning liquids).
Hand hygiene should be performed before putting on gloves.
Remove your gloves after caring for the patient.
Do not wear the same pair of gloves to care for multiple patients.
When gloves are worn, gloves should be removed and hand hygiene performed if movement is made from a contaminated body site to another uncontaminated body site (including mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or medical devices) during patient care.
An example is to start with wound dressings (non-complete skin) on the abdomen, followed by a vein cut on the arm.
Wash hands after removing gloves.
What is important is that hand contamination often occurs during glove removal due to poor technology or the absence of pinholes on gloves.
A hand not clean may spread microbes throughout the glove box.
That is why hand hygiene should be performed both before and after putting on gloves as one of the reasons.
To learn more about glove usage and other types of personal protective equipment (PPE), consider registering our personal protective equipment module.
Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water.
Washing hands with soap and water is intended to remove dirt, organic substances, and microorganisms on the hands.
Soap helps mechanical removal of debris, loosely attached microorganisms, and substances containing fat and oil, which are typically found on dirty hands.
While friction generated by washing hands with regular soap and water can remove loosely attached transient microbiota, the antibacterial activity of regular soap, if present, is extremely low.
The correct duration for washing hands is important – 30 seconds can remove ten times more bacteria than 15 seconds.
If correct, the entire hand washing process takes 40 to 60 seconds.
In the following situations, it is recommended to wash hands with soap and water (rather than using alcohol-based hand rub).
If hands have obvious stains or are contaminated with blood or other body fluids.
When suspecting or confirming exposure to pathogenic organisms that form spores, including Clostridium difficile (a bacterial infection that causes severe diarrhea), because friction generated by washing hands with soap and water can physically remove the spores from the hands.

Use alcohol-based hand rub to clean your hands.
If hands are not visibly dirty, alcohol-based hand rub is the preferred method for cleaning hands because ABHR is more effective than regular or antibacterial soap and water in killing microorganisms, and it has other significant advantages.
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) kills microbes by denaturing proteins through dissolving certain microbial components.
It also has persistent activity, meaning microorganisms need longer time to multiply on hands.
Using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) to clean hands is superior to using soap and water, including:
Less time required – just 20 to 30 seconds.
Using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) in a busy work environment can save your time!
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can be directly placed at the care point, making it convenient and easy to perform hand hygiene during clinical workflows.
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) does not require a sink, water, or towels – hands can dry naturally.

Nails, nail polish, and jewelry
Long nails, nail polish, and jewelry affect hand hygiene and promote bacterial growth.
Cuticle care or nail polish under jewelry leaves difficult spaces to clean and hinders access to these areas.
Natural nails should be kept relatively short, not exceeding 0.5 centimeters (less than 1/4 inch) beyond the fingertip.
When working in the clinical area, artificial nails or acrylic extensions are not recommended.
If you paint your nails, do not wear them down.
Strongly advised not to wear jewelry including rings.

One should be cautious in using hand creams and hand lotions.

Hand cream, hand lotion, or moisturizing skin care products can be used to reduce dryness and contact dermatitis caused by frequent hand washing leading to hand hygiene.

5.3 Exercise to Evaluate a Tuberculosis Laboratory Network
Given her enrollment in this online course, it is assumed that she is part of the apex of the pyramid of the tuberculosis laboratory networks hierarchy.
For example, you can be a consultant working at the national reference laboratory or provide support to national programs on the adoption of new diagnostic methods or strengthening the TB laboratory.
In evaluating a network of laboratories from the perspective of introducing new diagnostic methods, it is important to have a precise understanding of the tests being performed, at what level, for which groups, and the integrity of diagnostic services provision.
As an exercise, you could consider the following questions about a laboratory network that you know well. This is not a complete list.
Are the PDRs recommended by the WHO (e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP, or Truenat) available in peripheral laboratories, either directly or through a sample referral system?
Are molecular sensitivity tests used as initial tests to detect drug resistance in samples that are positive by smear microscopy or isolates obtained through cultures of the complex Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTBC), instead of using phenotypic sensitivity tests based on cultivation?
Is the LAM-ICL test used in urine as a diagnostic aid for active TB in adults, adolescents, and children, who are positive for HIV and have TB signs and symptoms?
Is cultivation performed during the follow-up of patients with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis or multidrug-resistant tuberculosis?
Are samples being sent to other laboratories for subsequent tests?
Is it done properly (that is, for the right tests and efficiently)?
Is a WHO-recommended PDR conducted in at least 90% of reported new and relapsed TB cases as initial diagnostic test?
If you answered no to any of the previous questions, the structure of the TB laboratory network is not optimal or it is necessary to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
