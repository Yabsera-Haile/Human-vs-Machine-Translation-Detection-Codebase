Hand hygiene is a general term applicable to washing hands, hand rubbing (such as using an alcohol-based hand rub) or surgical hand disinfection.
Hand hygiene is a simple but important procedure, yet even healthcare workers often overlook its significance.
Effective hand hygiene protection for patients, healthcare workers, and the healthcare environment from harmful pathogens.
By following suggested hand hygiene practices, we can help protect patients either by preventing infections from occurring or preventing the spread of existing ones.
Therefore, simple methods like timely or effective hand hygiene can reduce the number of patients with healthcare-associated infections (HAI).
The reduction of HAI means fewer resources are used to treat preventable infections, ultimately reducing incidence and mortality rates.
Reducing HAI will also reduce the use of antibiotics, thereby lowering antibiotic resistance.
Therefore, hand hygiene is an indispensable health care measure that can save lives and money!

Five Moments of Hand Hygiene
Healthcare workers should practice hand hygiene in the 5 moments as recommended by WHO.
WHO places great emphasis on contact that occurs *within the patient area* during patient care.
It presents a unified vision for all healthcare workers, trainers, and observers to enhance their understanding of when to clean their hands.
It combines all hand hygiene instructions recommended by the WHOs Hand Hygiene Guideline and the CDCs Guideline for Hand Hygiene in Healthcare Settings into the five moments when hand hygiene is needed.
Whats most important is that this user-centered approach aims to simplify usability and integrate it into natural workflows, making it applicable to a wide range of care environments and healthcare settings.
The patient area includes the patient and any surface or object temporarily belonging to him/her (as shown by dotted lines in Figure X-1 at 5 points in time).
This usually includes all non-living surfaces that the patient and those who have come into contact with or directly touched, such as bedside railings, bedside tables, bed items, intravenous tubes and other medical equipment.
Healthcare workers frequently come into contact with surfaces such as monitoring equipment, dials and buttons, and other touched surfaces when caring for patients.
Before touching a patient, please wash your hands to prevent the patient from being harmed by viruses carried on your hands.
Before coming into contact with areas of the patient that pose a risk of infection, immediately clean your hands to prevent the patient from being harmed by harmful bacteria, including the patients own harmful bacteria that may infect his/her own body.
Fluid contact risk afterwards
Touching the patients bodily fluids (including after removing gloves) should be cleaned immediately.
This action will protect you and the medical environment from being invaded by pathogens in the blood and bodily fluids.
Touch the patient and the surrounding environment, and when leaving the patients side, clean your hands to prevent yourself and the healthcare environment from being invaded by harmful microorganisms.
Even if you do not have direct contact with the patient, wash your hands after touching any object or furniture near the patient to prevent harmful microorganisms from spreading from the patient area to other parts of the medical environment.

Using gloves is an important tool for protecting hands from contamination.
Wearing gloves by medical workers does not mean neglecting hand hygiene.
In other words, wearing gloves cannot replace hand hygiene.
Hand hygiene and glove usage precautions include:
When handling potentially infectious substances or chemicals (such as disinfectant), wear gloves when coming into contact with blood or other bodily fluids, mucous membranes, or incomplete skin.
Before putting on gloves, wash your hands.
After taking care of the patient, please remove your gloves.
Do not use the same pair of gloves to care for multiple patients.
When wearing gloves, if you move from a contaminated body part to another uncontaminated body part (including mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or medical equipment of the same patient or environment), you should remove the gloves and perform hand hygiene during patient care.
An example is to apply dressing to the wound on the abdomen (not intact skin), then perform venipuncture on the arm.
Remove gloves and perform hand hygiene.
Whats key is to know that due to poor technology or small holes not being found on the gloves, hand contamination often occurs during the glove removal process.
Inserting a dirty hand into a glove box may spread microorganisms throughout the box.
This is why hand hygiene must be done before and after putting on gloves.
To learn more about glove use and other types of personal protective equipment (PPE), consider registering our personal protective products module.
Wash hands thoroughly with soap and clean water.
The purpose of washing your hands with soap and water is to remove dirt, organic matter, and microorganisms from your hands.
Soap helps to mechanically remove flakes, loosen and detach microorganisms that are loosely attached, and substances containing fat and oil, which are commonly found on dirty hands.
Although using regular soap and water to rub hands can remove loose attached transient microbial populations, the antibacterial activity of regular soap is extremely low even if it exists.
Proper hand-washing time is crucial - the amount of bacteria removed in 30 seconds is 10 times that of 15 seconds.
If correct, the entire hand-washing process requires 40 to 60 seconds.
In the following situations, it is recommended to wash your hands with soap and water (and not use hand sanitizer).
If both hands are visibly soiled or contaminated with blood or other bodily fluids.
When suspecting or confirming exposure to spore-forming pathogens, such as Clostridium difficile (a bacterium that can cause severe diarrhea), the friction generated by washing hands with soap and water can physically remove spores from the hands.

Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR)
If hands are not visibly soiled, alcohol-based hand rubs are the preferred method for cleaning hands, as alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) are more effective in killing microorganisms than ordinary or antibacterial soap and water, and have many other significant advantages.
Ethanol in Alcohol-Based Hand Rub (ABHR) can kill microorganisms by denaturing proteins, thereby dissolving certain components of the microorganisms.
It still has persistent activity, meaning that microorganisms need a longer time to multiply on the hands.
Using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) to clean hands is superior to using soap and water in that
Requires less time – only 20 to 30 seconds.
In a busy work environment, using alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can save your time!
Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) can be placed at the nursing stations, making hand hygiene easy and convenient in the clinical workflow.
Alcohol-Based Hand Rub (ABHR) does not need a sink, water, or towel – hands can air dry.
Note that the active ingredient – alcohol – is flammable, so you need to be careful and ensure that alcohol-based hand rubs (ABHR) are stored away from heat sources.

Nails, nail polish and jewelry
Long fingernails, nail polish and jewelry can affect hand hygiene and promote the growth of bacteria.
Nail polish or long nail tips and space between jewelry and the nail bed are difficult to clean and obstruct access to these areas.
Natural fingernails should be kept relatively short, ideally not exceeding 0.5 centimeters beyond the tip of the finger (less than 1/4 inch).
When working in clinical areas, it is not recommended to use artificial fingernails or extended nail tips.

5.3 Exercise to Evaluate a TB Laboratory Network
Given your enrollment in this online course, it is assumed that you are part of the pinnacle of the TB laboratory network pyramid.
For example, it can be a consultant who works at the national reference laboratory or who provides support to national programs on the adoption of new diagnostic methods or the strengthening of the TB laboratory.
Evaluating a network of laboratories from the perspective of the need to introduce new diagnostic methods, it is essential to have a precise understanding of the tests performed, at what level, for what groups, and the integrity of the provision of diagnostic services.
As an exercise, you might pose the following questions about a network of laboratories you are familiar with. This is not an exhaustive list.
Are the Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, TB-LAMP or Truenat (WHO-recommended PDRs, for example) available in peripheral laboratories, either directly or through a sample referral system?
Are molecular drug susceptibility tests used as the initial test to detect drug resistance in samples with positive smear or isolates obtained by culturing the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, instead of using phenotypic drug susceptibility tests based on culture?
Is the LAM-ICL test used in urine as an aid in the diagnosis of active TB in adults, adolescents, and children, HIV-positive with signs and symptoms of TB?
Is cultivation performed during the follow-up of patients with rifampicin-resistant TB or multidrug-resistant TB?
Are the samples sent to other laboratories for consecutive testing?
Is this done properly (that is, for the correct tests) and efficiently?
Is a recommended TB test by WHO performed as an initial diagnostic test in at least 90% of new and relapse TB cases notified?
If you answered no to any of the previous questions, the TB laboratory network structure is not optimal or it is necessary to establish or expand the use of new diagnostic methods.
